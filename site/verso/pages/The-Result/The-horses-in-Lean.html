1.15. The horses in Lean🔗

Terms such as library, claim, condition and frame are explained under The Result.

This is the case set out under The horses, built in Lean. It does a second job at the same time. It shows that the law, in its chain form, is not empty. A theorem is not empty when all its claims and conditions can hold at once. If they could not, the theorem would be true only because nothing ever meets them. Rothbard's own six horses meet them all. The man's plan satisfies swap dominance (SwapDominant, explained under The finding). And the horses meet every condition of the law's chain form, the form along a chain of named horses (marginal_utility_chain, under The theorems).

Wants are ranks on the man's value scale. The smaller rank number is the more urgent want:

🔗def
Apodictic.Model.rankPrefers (X Y : Set Model.Want) : Prop
Apodictic.Model.rankPrefers
  (X Y : Set Model.Want) : Prop

Preference between bundles of wants. A bundle is a set of wants. X is preferred to Y exactly when two things hold. First, the two bundles differ by a single swap: X has one want Y lacks, Y has one want X lacks, and otherwise they are the same. Second, the want only X has is the more urgent of the two. Here more urgent just means a smaller rank number.

The man's plan is the obvious one. With any n horses he would serve the n most urgent wants. This plan satisfies swap dominance (SwapDominant). The proof is the last theorem on this page. Five more things hold here. The first three are the chain form's conditions:

  • Wants can be told apart (DecidableEq praxis.End). For any two wants, whether they are the same want can be decided.

  • Uses are independent (IndependentUses agent time, under The conditions). Here the horses' jobs do not complement one another.

  • One-horse steps exist inside the stable (stock.OneMore). A set of n of his horses lies inside a set of n + 1, and both lie inside the stable. The chain form takes two such steps, one after the other: from n horses to n + 1, then from n + 1 to n + 2. Its conclusion compares them. Every want the first step adds is preferred to every want the second step adds.

The other two are not conditions of the chain form:

  • Each step has a marginal end (horses_marginal_nonempty). Going from n horses to n + 1, the plan serves one more want, rank n + 1. That want is the step's marginal end: served with the larger set, not with the smaller. The law's conclusion speaks of every want a step adds. So this shows it speaks of actual wants here, not of none.

  • Preference is asymmetric (AsymmetricPreference).

Asymmetry is a claim, one of The claims. If he prefers one bundle to another, he does not prefer the second to the first. It is the strict reading of preference, and preference is meant as strict throughout. The chain form does not take it. It is proved of this frame (horses_asymmetric) for another reason. It shows that the two claims can hold together here: swap dominance (SwapDominant) and asymmetry. The rival-plan theorem (no_rival_swap_dominant, under The theorems) takes both. It says this. Take a rival plan for the same stable. At one set of horses, it differs from his by a single swap. Then the rival does not also satisfy swap dominance. The rival is constrained at that one set of horses only. No theorem here applies the rival-plan theorem to the horses.

Most theorems here only check that the horses meet one hypothesis or another. Two do more. They are conclusions Rothbard reached himself. The first is his result at p. 25:

🔗theorem
Apodictic.Model.loss_of_a_horse_ends_pleasure_riding :
  Model.pleasureRiding ∈
    marginalEnds (Model.horsePlan 6) (Finset.range 5) (Finset.range 6)
Apodictic.Model.loss_of_a_horse_ends_pleasure_riding :
  Model.pleasureRiding ∈
    marginalEnds (Model.horsePlan 6)
      (Finset.range 5) (Finset.range 6)

Rothbard's own conclusion, p. 25. With six horses the man would serve the six most urgent wants. With five he would not serve the sixth-ranked want. So the sixth-ranked want is one he gives up when a horse goes. In Rothbard's telling it is pleasure riding: "the loss of a horse will cause him to give up pleasure riding".

Here it is a calculation about the plan, not a further assumption.

The second is the point he names two horses to make. It is also the reason the plan is encoded the way it is:

🔗theorem
Apodictic.Model.which_horse_does_not_matter :
  (Model.horsePlan 6).wouldServe
      ((Finset.range 6).erase Model.manOWar) =
    (Model.horsePlan 6).wouldServe
      ((Finset.range 6).erase Model.seabiscuit)
Apodictic.Model.which_horse_does_not_matter :
  (Model.horsePlan 6).wouldServe
      ((Finset.range 6).erase
        Model.manOWar) =
    (Model.horsePlan 6).wouldServe
      ((Finset.range 6).erase
        Model.seabiscuit)

Rothbard's own conclusion, p. 27. It is the one he names two horses to make. Man o' War was leading a wagon. Seabiscuit was out for pleasure riding. After losing Man o' War, the man would serve exactly the ends he would serve after losing Seabiscuit. In Rothbard's telling, he simply moves Seabiscuit to the wagon. Which of the two goes makes no difference to which end is given up.

This is what interchangeability amounts to: the proof is interchangeability of units (horses_homogeneous) applied to the two sets of five horses left. It is also why the plan is encoded as it is. The plan (AllocationPlan) takes a set of horses, not a number. It gives the ends he would serve with exactly those horses. So the two sets of five are two different inputs, and they could give different ends. That any two sets of the same size give the same ends is a separate named condition (Homogeneous), and it can fail (unitsAlike_not_entail_homogeneous). A plan that took only a number would build Rothbard's point in, so that it could not fail. Rothbard's point has to be statable before it can be true.

And the last step is the point:

🔗theorem
Apodictic.Model.horses_law_applies (k n : ℕ) (h : n < k)
  (h' : n + 1 < k) (addedFirst : Model.Horses.End) :
  addedFirst ∈
      marginalEnds (Model.horsePlan k) (Finset.range n)
        (Finset.range (n + 1)) →
    ∀
      addedSecond ∈
        marginalEnds (Model.horsePlan k) (Finset.range (n + 1))
          (Finset.range (n + 2)),
      Model.Horses.PrefersEnd () () addedFirst addedSecond
Apodictic.Model.horses_law_applies
  (k n : ℕ) (h : n < k) (h' : n + 1 < k)
  (addedFirst : Model.Horses.End) :
  addedFirst ∈
      marginalEnds (Model.horsePlan k)
        (Finset.range n)
        (Finset.range (n + 1)) →
    ∀
      addedSecond ∈
        marginalEnds (Model.horsePlan k)
          (Finset.range (n + 1))
          (Finset.range (n + 2)),
      Model.Horses.PrefersEnd () ()
        addedFirst addedSecond

The law itself, applied to the horses. Every hypothesis of marginal_utility_chain is met at this frame. The claim is swap dominance (SwapDominant), proved by horses_swapDominant. The conditions are that wants can be told apart, independence of uses (horses_independent) and the two one-horse steps (horses_one_more). Lean accepts this proof only because each hypothesis is supplied with a proof of it. So the claim and the conditions can all hold at once, and the law is not empty. That is the whole argument that they are consistent.

That settles the chain form. The size-based form, by the size of the supply (marginal_utility, under The theorems), asks for one thing more: interchangeability (plan.Homogeneous). The horses meet that too:

🔗theorem
Apodictic.Model.horses_homogeneous (k : ℕ) :
  (Model.horsePlan k).Homogeneous
Apodictic.Model.horses_homogeneous
  (k : ℕ) :
  (Model.horsePlan k).Homogeneous

Interchangeability holds of the plan. The plan takes a set of horses. The ends it gives depend only on how many horses are in the set, never on which.

But no theorem here applies the size-based form to them. That would take one more theorem, and it has not been built. So this page shows only the chain form not empty.

One step above was taken on trust: that the man's plan satisfies swap dominance. Here is its proof. horses_swapDominant proves SwapDominant (horsePlan k), where horsePlan k is the man's plan for a stable of k horses. That is exactly the hypothesis marginal_utility_chain takes (dominance), and horses_law_applies hands it over as it stands. So nothing was copied across by hand. A hand copy would be a second statement of swap dominance, written out for the horses. It could differ from the hypothesis the law takes. Then the horses would be shown to meet something other than that hypothesis. Here there is no second statement.

🔗theorem
Apodictic.Model.horses_swapDominant (k : ℕ) :
  SwapDominant (Model.horsePlan k)
Apodictic.Model.horses_swapDominant
  (k : ℕ) :
  SwapDominant (Model.horsePlan k)

The man's plan is swap-dominant. This is the claim itself, proved of this frame and this plan. Serving the n most urgent wants beats any one-swap alternative. That is because a want a horse can serve, but the plan would leave out, is less urgent than every want the plan would serve. It has a larger rank number.

The model departs from Rothbard in two places. Both are said out loud rather than papered over.

  • No ceiling on the scale. His ten ends are "for simplicity", to fit a diagram. A ceiling would drag an extra condition through every theorem, and it would gain nothing: without one, his six horses still serve the first six ends, as in his case. So here the horses can serve any rank.

  • Which earlier horse is Man o' War is arbitrary. Rothbard calls Seabiscuit "the sixth horse that he had previously acquired". He says only that Man o' War "had arrived earlier" (MES p. 27). So any earlier horse will do. Here the horses are numbered 0 to 5. Seabiscuit is horse 5 and Man o' War is horse 1. What the plan serves depends only on how many horses there are (horses_homogeneous). A horse's number serves only to tell horses apart. Nothing else about a horse enters any theorem here.
