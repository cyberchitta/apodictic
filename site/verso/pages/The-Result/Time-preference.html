1.12. Time preference🔗

Time preference is the third target. Like exchange, it was chosen for what it can diagnose. The claim (TimePreference) is this: a man prefers a satisfaction sooner to the same satisfaction later, if the sooner is not already past. Rothbard builds interest on it. Both authors state it as universal. Rothbard: "A fundamental and constant truth about human action is that man prefers his end to be achieved in the shortest possible time. Given the specific satisfaction, the sooner it arrives, the better" (MES, p. 15). Mises: "Satisfaction of a want in the nearer future is, other things being equal, preferred to that in the farther distant future", and "Time preference is a categorial requisite of human action" (Human Action, ch. XVIII, §2). Each of them attaches a qualifier. Rothbard's is "given the specific satisfaction". Mises's is "other things being equal". Everything turns on these qualifiers, and on who decides when they are met.

Dates. Until now the vocabulary had no "sooner". Time was not ordered, and an end had no date. That was because no theorem asked for either. Time preference cannot be stated without them. So the vocabulary grows here, and only here. Three things are added:

  • Times get a bare relation, "before" (Before). It has no properties at all.

  • Each end gets the time it is attained at (attained).

  • A relation says which pairs of ends are the same satisfaction at two dates, for a given man (SameSatisfaction).

Who decides "the same satisfaction". Rothbard's footnote defines time preference as "the preference for present satisfaction over future satisfaction or present good over future good, provided it is remembered that it is the same satisfaction (or “good”) that is being compared". It then meets the obvious objection: "Since ice-in-the-summer provides different (and greater) satisfactions than ice-in-the-winter, they are not the same, but different goods" (pp. 15–16, n. 15). On that reading, how the man values two dated ends settles whether they are the same satisfaction. Then whenever he prefers the later one, it was a different good. So the claim is never tested.

Mises sets the same case aside on another ground. He calls it "the case of perishable goods". Winter ice is "a good that loses its specific usefulness even if not consumed". Summer ice is "another good which requires a different process of production". So the two are "for all practical purposes different commodities" (Human Action, ch. XVIII, §2). Neither description is a valuation. Both can be checked without asking what the man prefers: winter ice serves in summer only through a special process of conservation, and summer ice needs a different process of production. Rothbard's ground cannot.

Lean shows exactly what Rothbard's reading buys. Define "the same satisfaction" as "the later is not preferred to the sooner" (SameByValuation). Then one half of time preference holds in every frame: the later is never preferred. That half carries no claim at all:

On the valuation reading, time preference's negative half is free. Take any frame, agent and date. Take two ends that are the same satisfaction by valuation. Then the later is never preferred. No claim is carried: the signature has none. That is because the sentence was made true by what "same" was allowed to mean.

The strict half is NOT free on this reading: that the sooner IS preferred. Take two ends, neither preferred to the other. The valuation reading counts them the same satisfaction. Time preference then asserts a preference that is not there. So on this reading the whole content of the claim is one thing: of two ends, one before the other and the sooner not yet past, the pair is never left unranked. That is comparability, and neither author argues for it.

The other half is not free: that the sooner is preferred. Take a frame in which nothing is ranked. It satisfies the valuation reading for every pair. And there the sooner is not preferred:

On the valuation reading, the strict half of time preference is not free. It fails where nothing is ranked. There every pair counts as the same satisfaction by valuation. And the sooner is not preferred.

So, on Rothbard's reading, time preference asserts one thing. Take two ends, one before the other, the sooner not yet past. One is ranked above the other. Neither author argues for that. And it sits badly with a doctrine of Rothbard's own: indifference cannot be demonstrated in action. On this reading the claim rules out one case: two dated ends, neither preferred to the other. That is exactly the case no act of the man's could ever show.

The library therefore takes the other road. Sameness is supplied separately from the ranking. It is the man's, since satisfactions are his. But it is not read off his preferences. So the claim can fail. Say a man ranks the same satisfaction higher later. He is then a counterexample to the claim, not a case of a different good.

Time preference — of two ends that are the same satisfaction, the agent at now prefers the one attained sooner. This holds provided the sooner one is not already past.

Source: Rothbard, MES, ch. 1, p. 15: "A fundamental and constant truth about human action is that man prefers his end to be achieved in the shortest possible time. Given the specific satisfaction, the sooner it arrives, the better." / "This is the universal fact of time preference." Mises, Human Action, ch. XVIII, §2: "Satisfaction of a want in the nearer future is, other things being equal, preferred to that in the farther distant future." / "Time preference is a categorial requisite of human action."

Status: explicit-in-tradition as doctrine; our-reconstruction as a claim. Rothbard fixes "the same satisfaction" by the satisfaction it gives. Ice in summer and ice in winter "are not the same, but different goods" (MES pp. 15–16, n. 15). Read that way, the sentence cannot fail: whatever is preferred later was a different good. Mises sets ice aside on another ground, as "the case of perishable goods". Winter ice is "a good that loses its specific usefulness even if not consumed". Summer ice is "another good which requires a different process of production" (Human Action, ch. XVIII, §2). Neither is a fact about the ranking. Here SameSatisfaction is supplied apart from Prefers. So the claim constrains the ranking. An agent who prefers the same satisfaction later refutes it. The reading on which it cannot fail is built as the rejected encoding (Apodictic.TimePreference.valuation_reading_is_free). Carried by Apodictic.TimePreference.less_durable_preferred and by the interest theorems in Apodictic.PureInterest.

Does not say:

  1. Anything about ends that are not the same satisfaction. "Other things being equal" is carried whole by SameSatisfaction. A perishable good gives a pair that is not the same satisfaction. So do two enjoyments that cannot be had together (Mises's Carmen and Hamlet). So does unequal certainty of getting it. The claim is silent on each. It is silent on anticipation too. A pleasure put off brings the looking forward with it, from now until it comes. A pain got over with spares the waiting for it. Either way, the later option holds a satisfaction that begins now, and the earlier does not. That is the literature's own account: savouring is "positive utility derived from anticipation of future consumption", dread "negative utility resulting from contemplation of the future" (Loewenstein, Economic Journal 1987, p. 667). It counts as a diagnosis only where the anticipation is shown apart from the choice it explains. Inferred from the choice to wait, it is the valuation reading again.

  2. Anything about bundles. Preferring a sooner bundle needs DatedFrame.LiftsOverRest.

  3. Anything about ends already past at now.

  4. That Before is transitive, irreflexive or total. The claim uses "sooner" only as a label on the pair.

  5. How MUCH sooner is better. No rate, no discounting.

  6. That the preference shows in any act. That bridge is DemonstratedTimePreference, of one act.

Durability. Rothbard draws one consequence at once: "if the actor values the total service rendered by two consumers' goods equally, he will, because of time preference, choose the less durable good" (pp. 16–17, n. 16). Its smallest case is two goods whose services differ only in the date of one service. There it follows, at a price:

Two goods render the same services rest, plus one service more. The first renders it at the earlier date (soon). The second renders it at the later date (late). "Values the total service equally" is read as two things. The two services are the same satisfaction. And everything else is the same rest.

The conclusion is a preference, not a choice. No claim in the library runs from the ranking to which good the agent picks.

Rothbard gives no example of this consequence. Here is one, and it is ours. Two season tickets cover the same nine concerts (rest). The first also covers a tenth concert on Friday (soon). The second covers the same concert, repeated on Sunday (late). The conclusion: the man now prefers the first ticket to the second. It costs one claim and four conditions:

  • Time preference (TimePreference), the claim. It fails if he does not prefer the Friday concert to the Sunday one, though the next three conditions hold.

  • The two concerts are the same satisfaction for him (SameSatisfaction). It fails if Sunday's has another soloist, say.

  • Friday comes before Sunday (sooner). It fails if the repeat is on Thursday.

  • Friday is not already past (notPast). It fails if it is Saturday now.

  • Preferring Friday's concert carries up to preferring the whole first ticket (LiftsOverRest). It can fail if Friday's concert clashes with one of the nine. Say one of the nine is also on Friday evening. Then the second ticket may be better, though Friday's concert alone is preferred.

The price is the last condition. Time preference ranks single satisfactions. The goods deliver streams of them. Preferring the sooner service is one thing. Preferring the stream it belongs to is another. To get from the one to the other, the rest of the stream must stay out of it. That fails where the sooner delivery clashes with something else in the stream. Rothbard's sentence does not show this condition. Independence of uses (IndependentUses agent time, under The conditions) carries a preference down, from bundles to ends. This condition carries one up, from ends to bundles:

Lifts over the rest — a condition on the situation, NOT a universal claim. Preferring one end to another carries UP. It becomes preferring the bundle rest with the one to rest with the other.

It is the converse direction of IndependentUses, fixed to one pair and one rest. It fails for complements and clashes. Say a sooner delivery collides with something else in rest: two services due at one moment. Then the bundle with the later one can be better, though the sooner one alone is preferred.

Two things are not reached:

  • More than one service at a different date. The preferences would then have to be chained. That needs transitivity of preference, which nothing here asserts.

  • Rothbard's "will choose". The conclusion is a preference. No claim here runs from what a man ranks to which of two goods he picks. Only one claim in the library runs from ranking to act (ActsInOrder). It is about how a stock is allocated, not about this.

Mises's regress. Mises does not only assert time preference. He argues that action is impossible without it: "If he were not to prefer satisfaction in a nearer period of the future to that in a remoter period, he would never consume and so satisfy wants. ... He would not consume today, but he would not consume tomorrow either, as the morrow would confront him with the same alternative." The argument runs from what a man prefers to what he does. So it cannot be stated without a bridge between the two. Mises supplies one, in the same section. It runs the other way round, from the act to the preference. That is the same link read backwards. If consuming shows a preference for the sooner, then without that preference there is no consuming that shows one. Mises: "He who consumes a nonperishable good instead of postponing consumption for an indefinite later moment thereby reveals a higher valuation of present satisfaction". That sentence is the second new claim (DemonstratedTimePreference). It is stated of one act:

Demonstrated time preference — an act that takes a satisfaction and forgoes the same satisfaction later shows that the agent prefers the sooner, at the time of acting.

This is the bridge from an act to the ranking, for acts across dates. DemonstratedPreference is that bridge for trades. It is asserted of the act handed to a theorem, not of every act.

Source: Mises, Human Action, ch. XVIII, §2: "He who consumes a nonperishable good instead of postponing consumption for an indefinite later moment thereby reveals a higher valuation of present satisfaction". Rothbard, MES, p. 307: "Any action demonstrates choice based on preference: preference for one alternative over others."

Status: explicit-in-tradition. Mises's regress runs on a premise it does not state: that an agent with no preference for the sooner postpones. This claim is that premise read the other way round (Apodictic.TimePreference.consumption_reveals). It is carried by Apodictic.TimePreference.consumption_reveals and by Mises's regress (Apodictic.TimePreference.regress).

Does not say:

  1. Anything about acts that forgo something other than the same satisfaction later. "Nonperishable" is carried by SameSatisfaction.

  2. That the chosen satisfaction is present, rather than merely sooner.

  3. Anything about other acts or other times.

The morrow's alternative — Mises's regress, as data. At each date in a sequence the agent can have a satisfaction now. Or he can have the same satisfaction at the next date: "the morrow would confront him with the same alternative" (Human Action, ch. XVIII, §2).

It is a structure because the regress is about one such sequence. Its Prop fields fix only the dates: which offer belongs to which date, and that each date comes before the next. That each offer is the same satisfaction as the next is what makes it the SAME alternative. It can fail: the good perishes. So it is not a field. It is the named condition Morrows.SameAlternative.

No time preference carries to the morrow — a condition on the situation. Say on some date the agent does not prefer the offer now to the offer on the morrow. Then he does not on the next date either.

This is constancy of the scale, cut to the one thing the regress spends: the ABSENCE of a preference, for this sequence, from one date to the next. Both authors deny constancy in general (Rothbard: scales "differ for the same individual at different times", MES p. 17).

Mises's regress (Human Action, ch. XVIII, §2): "If he were not to prefer satisfaction in a nearer period of the future to that in a remoter period, he would never consume and so satisfy wants. ... He would not consume today, but he would not consume tomorrow either, as the morrow would confront him with the same alternative."

It is stated from the act. Take an act of consuming at any date of the sequence. If it demonstrates, it refutes "no preference for the sooner at the first date". What the argument spends is in the signature:

  • the demonstration (Mises's "reveals", in the same section);

  • that each day's offer is the same satisfaction as the next (his "the same alternative");

  • that the absence of preference carries from date to date.

The conclusion is a double negation. The argument refutes the absence of a preference at the first date. It does not produce the preference. That follows from how Mises words the step. He carries the ABSENCE of a preference forward ("he would not consume tomorrow either"). So does NoPreferenceCarries. Getting the preference itself back from that needs excluded middle: at the first date he either prefers the sooner or does not. The library does not assume excluded middle. The condition could be carried the other way instead, as a preference passed back from each date to the one before. Then it would give the preference directly.

Mises's own case runs through the theorem. A man holds a nonperishable good. Today he can consume it, or keep it for tomorrow. Tomorrow offers him the same choice. Say that on some day he consumes it. The conclusion: it is not the case that, on the first day, he had no preference for the sooner. It costs one claim and three conditions:

  • Consuming reveals a preference for the sooner (DemonstratedTimePreference), the claim. It fails if he consumes without preferring the sooner.

  • On that day he consumes: he takes the day's offer and forgoes the next day's (ConsumesAt). It fails if he keeps the good that day.

  • Each day's offer is the same satisfaction as the next (SameAlternative). This is Mises's "the same alternative", and his "nonperishable". It fails if the good perishes. Mises's winter ice fails it. That ice serves in summer only after a process of conservation.

  • Having no preference for the sooner carries from each day to the next (NoPreferenceCarries). It fails if, after a day on which he does not prefer the sooner, he comes to prefer it the next day.

Three findings are in this:

  • The regress and the "reveals" sentence are one premise, used twice. The premise is that consuming the sooner shows a preference for it. The regress passage leaves it unstated. The "reveals" sentence states it. Once it is stated, it does all the work. The regress contributes only the carrying from day to day.

  • What comes out is far short of the universal. It is a preference at one date, for one sequence of offers. It is not "the sooner is better" for every end at every date.

  • It comes out only as a double negation. Mises carries the absence of a preference forward. So the argument refutes his having no preference on the first day. This argument yields the preference itself only with excluded middle. The library does not assume excluded middle.

Waiting is a frame where a man prefers each satisfaction sooner. In it, both claims and every condition of both theorems hold together. The test is weak in two places, and they are worth saying:

  • The durability case is handed an empty rest of the stream. So the lifting condition (LiftsOverRest) is never tried on a real one.

  • There is no absence of preference for the regress's carrying condition (NoPreferenceCarries) to carry. So it holds with nothing to do.

Non-vacuity: the regress applies in Waiting. There the agent prefers the sooner at every date. So no absence of preference has anything to carry.

The carried conditions. The regress's manifest lists two fields of the sequence. Each day's offer is attained on that day. Each day comes before the next. It also lists three fields of the act. Each of the five is of a kind that assumes nothing about the world. Take a sequence whose "offer on the third day" arrives on some other day. Or take an act that gives nothing up. Neither is a situation that might obtain. That each day's offer is the same satisfaction as the next is not of that kind. The good may perish. So it is not a field. It is the named condition above (SameAlternative).

What "before" was asked for. Nothing. No proof here uses any property of "before". None uses that it is transitive. None uses even that no time comes before itself. "Sooner" enters every statement as a label on a pair of dates. It is never a step in an argument. Lean was expected to force an order on time here. It did not.

What a failure would point at. Say a man prefers a satisfaction later. Then one of these failed:

  • The two dated ends were the same satisfaction for him (SameSatisfaction). It fails where the good perishes, or the enjoyments cannot be had together. It fails where the later is surer, or the wait itself is worth something to him.

  • The sooner one was not already past (notPast).

  • The claim itself (TimePreference).

Say he prefers the less durable good less. Then one of the same failed. Or the sooner service clashed with the rest of the stream (LiftsOverRest). Say the regress does not bite. Then one of these failed:

  • The offer was the same from day to day (SameAlternative).

  • His lack of preference carried (NoPreferenceCarries).

  • Consuming revealed what Mises says it does (DemonstratedTimePreference).

The textbook case against the claim is on that list. People will pay more for a pleasure a few days off than for the same pleasure now. And they will pay more to avoid a pain that is put off: "Subjects on average were willing to pay more to experience a kiss delayed by 3 days than an immediate kiss or one delayed by three hours or one day" (Loewenstein, Economic Journal 1987, p. 668). Waiting for a pleasure is itself a pleasure. Waiting for a pain is itself a pain. So the later option carries a satisfaction that begins now. The pair falls outside the claim, which says nothing about it. The move has the shape of Rothbard's ice. One test separates a diagnosis from an escape. Can the difference be shown without looking at the choice? Summer heat can be. "Greater satisfactions", read off the preference, cannot. For dread it has been shown. People waiting for an electric shock were scanned. The scans told them apart: "Even when no decision was required, these extreme dreaders were distinguishable from those who dreaded mildly". And the extreme dreaders were the ones who, given the choice, "preferred to receive more voltage rather than wait" (Berns et al., Science 2006). Anticipation that is only inferred from the waiting brings back the reading on which the claim cannot fail.
