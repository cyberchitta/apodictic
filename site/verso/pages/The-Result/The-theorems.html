1.6. The theorems🔗

Rothbard's route is one of the two routes to the law (see The Result). On it the derivation has three steps:

  • Served over unserved (served_over_unserved). Every end the man would serve with a set of his horses is preferred to every end a horse could serve that he would leave unserved with that set.

  • Urgency (urgency_principle). Take a horse away. Every end he would still serve is preferred to every end he would give up.

  • The law of marginal utility. It comes in two forms: along a chain of named horses (marginal_utility_chain), and by the size of the supply (marginal_utility).

Only the first step does real work. Its proof is the only one that applies swap dominance (SwapDominant) and independence of uses, for one man at one time (IndependentUses agent time). Both are explained below. The later steps take both as hypotheses, only to hand them to the step before. Urgency is one application of the first step. The chain form is one application of urgency. The size form adds one thing first: interchangeability of units (plan.Homogeneous). It says any two sets of his horses of the same size would serve the same ends. After that, the size form too is urgency again.

Start with Rothbard's six horses, set out under The horses. There his ten ends are numbered by rank, in order of importance (pp. 25–26). With six horses the man serves ends 1–6. Ends 7–10 remain unsatisfied. A bundle is a set of ends. Take the bundle he serves, {1,2,3,4,5,6}. The claim (SwapDominant) says the following. Swap any served end for any unserved end a horse could have served. Then he prefers what he would have had to what the swap leaves him. So {1,2,3,4,5,6} beats {1,2,3,4,5,7}. It also beats {1,2,3,4,5,8}. And it beats {2,3,4,5,6,7} if end 1 is the one swapped out. Any single swap loses.

That claim is about bundles. The law is about single ends. Rothbard's case ranks pleasure riding below leading a wagon, "the more important duty (to him)" (p. 27). Pleasure riding is end 6. Rothbard gives leading a wagon no number. The point is that the law ranks one end against another, not one bundle against another. To pass from bundles to ends takes one more condition. Take two bundles that differ in exactly one place. A preference between them is then a preference between the two ends where they differ. That condition is independence of uses (IndependentUses agent time). It is ours rather than Rothbard's. He ranks ends against each other directly. He reads their ranking off his value scale (Figure 3, pp. 25–26). He takes that ranking as given. The passages quoted under The horses never compare bundles at all. We need the condition because we make a claim about bundles where he makes one fused claim. The condition fails where uses are complementary: where one end is worth having only alongside another. Each step of the derivation below carries the condition as a hypothesis. So where it fails, the law is silent. It says nothing there, rather than something false.

Add that condition, and the conclusion is served over unserved (served_over_unserved). Take any set of his horses (a sub-stock). Every end he would serve with it is preferred to every end a horse could serve that he would leave unserved with it. That is already more than Rothbard says. The conclusion does not care whether the unserved end is the one the next horse would have reached. End 6 beats end 7. It beats end 10 just as squarely.

Served over unserved — the workhorse. Take any sub-stock on hand. Every end the agent would serve with it is preferred to every end the good could serve but he would leave unserved.

It needs the hypothesis of independence of uses (IndependentUses agent time). That hypothesis is what carries a preference between two bundles down to a preference between two ends.

This theorem is everything SwapDominant gives about single ends. Swap dominance says nothing beyond its single swaps. Each swap it speaks of yields a preference between two bundles that differ in exactly one place. Independence of uses turns that into a preference between the two ends: the served end over the unserved one. This theorem covers every such swap: every sub-stock on hand, every served end, every unserved end the good could serve. It is more than Rothbard says. It sets a served end against ANY unserved end the good could serve, not only against the one the next unit would reach.

How the proof goes. Swap dominance says the served bundle beats the bundle with unserved swapped in for served. Write the served bundle as the end served together with the rest of the bundle (insert_sdiff_self_of_mem). The swapped bundle is the end unserved together with that same rest. Neither end is in the rest. served was taken out of it. unserved is not served at this sub-stock, and the rest is part of what is served there. So the two bundles differ in exactly one place. Independence of uses reads the preference between those two ends off that.

Urgency is that ranking, restricted to the end he loses. Take a horse away. The end he gives up is one he would have served with six and does not serve with five. So it is an unserved end at the smaller stock. Served-over-unserved has already ranked it below everything still served. Rothbard's sentence on losing a horse (p. 25, quoted under The horses) says exactly that and nothing more. The larger stock is exactly one horse larger (stock.OneMore fewer more). The proof uses only part of that: the smaller stock lies inside the larger, which lies inside the stock. So the smaller stock is on hand. That the larger has exactly one horse more is not used. The linter cannot flag that, because it judges each hypothesis whole (see The manifest).

The urgency principle (Rothbard), stated as a loss. Suppose the agent had more units and drops to fewer, one unit fewer (stock.OneMore fewer more). Every end he would still serve at fewer is preferred to every end he has to abandon on the way down. The loss falls on the least urgent want.

It is one application of served_over_unserved, at the sub-stock fewer. An abandoned end is one the plan would serve at more and not at fewer. So it is an end the good can serve, since the plan only ever puts a unit to an end the good is believed able to serve. That is a field of the plan itself (plan.servesOnlyWhatItCan), not a hypothesis. And the plan leaves it unserved at fewer. From stock.OneMore fewer more the proof uses only that fewer is on hand: it lies inside more, which lies inside the stock. That more has exactly one unit more than fewer is not used.

The law is urgency again, said of the ends a unit adds. These are the ends served with the larger stock and not with the smaller. That is Rothbard's own definition of the marginal utility of a supply.

  {praxis : ActionFrame}
  {agent : praxis.Agent}
  {time : praxis.Time}
  {stock : Stock praxis agent time}
  (plan : AllocationPlan stock)
  (fewer more : Finset praxis.Means) :
  Set praxis.End

Marginal utility of a one-unit step, from fewer to more, in Rothbard's own sense. It is the ends the extra unit adds. These are the same ends that would be given up if it were lost. "The marginal utility of the supply is the end that must be given up as the result of a loss of the unit" (MES p. 27); "he gives up the least urgent of the wants which the larger stock would have satisfied" (p. 25).

It is a set of ends rather than a single end, because nothing here assumes that exactly one end drops. Determinacy of the drop is not among the law's premises. It is a Set (a collection given by a condition on its members) rather than a Finset (a finite collection listed member by member) for a mechanical reason. Subtracting one Finset from another requires being able to decide when two ends are the same end. An arbitrary frame does not give us that. Lean's classical axiom (Classical.choice) would supply the test. No theorem outside Consistency.lean, the file of small frames, uses that axiom. Using it here would add it to the logical background of every theorem stated with this definition, for no praxeological reason at all.

It is a definition, not a claim. Rothbard introduces it as a definition ("is called", "is known as"). What licenses calling this the utility of the unit is imputation of value from ends back to means: "actors value means strictly in accordance with their valuation of the ends that they believe the means can serve" (p. 19).

It is indexed by the step rather than by a size. The reason is that which ends a unit adds can depend on which units are already on hand, unless interchangeability of units (plan.Homogeneous) holds.

Along a chain of named horses, take three sets of them: small inside medium inside large, each one horse more than the last. So there are two one-horse steps: small to medium, then medium to large. Apply urgency at the second of them. What the sixth horse adds is less urgent than what the fifth added. That is because what the fifth added is still being served when he has five, and what the sixth adds is not. That the first of the two is a one-horse step (stock.OneMore small medium) is not used. Interchangeability of units (plan.Homogeneous) is not needed either, because the chain names which horses.

The law of marginal utility, along a chain of named units. Take three sub-stocks, small inside medium inside large. Each is one unit more than the last (stock.OneMore). Every end the first step adds is preferred to every end the second step adds.

No interchangeability of units (plan.Homogeneous) is needed, because the chain names which units are involved. The theorem is one application of urgency_principle.

Two things go unused. The first is _firstStep. It is listed in the statement and does no work at all. The underscore records that. Each hypothesis that does no work is recorded as a finding. This one shows that the chain form needs nothing of how small stands to medium: not that the first step adds one unit, nor even that small lies inside medium. The second is half of a hypothesis: that an end is marginal at the first step, that is, among the ends that step adds (marginalEnds plan small medium). That has two halves. Only one is used: that the end is served at medium. That it is not served at small is not used. The proof records this by naming that half _notNeeded. That name is inside the proof, not in the statement.

The linter (#lint only unusedArguments) catches neither. The underscore silences it on _firstStep. And it judges each hypothesis whole, so it cannot see that half of one goes unused.

Said by the size of the supply, the law compares what a fifth horse adds with what a sixth adds. The two one-horse steps need not share a horse. The fifth runs from belowSmaller to smaller, a set of five. The sixth runs from belowLarger, a set of five that need not be the same one, to larger. Interchangeability of units (plan.Homogeneous) is used exactly once: to say that smaller and belowLarger would serve the same ends. After that, the argument is urgency over again.

The law of marginal utility (Rothbard, MES, ch. 1, p. 27): "The greater the supply of a good, the lower the marginal utility; the smaller the supply, the higher the marginal utility."

This is the version stated by supply SIZE. The first step runs from belowSmaller to smaller, which has n units. The second runs from belowLarger to larger, which has n + 1. Every end that is marginal at a supply of n units (one the first step adds) is preferred to every end marginal at a supply of n + 1 (one the second step adds). That holds for ANY two one-unit steps reaching those sizes. The two steps need not have a single unit in common.

Interchangeability of units (plan.Homogeneous) is needed exactly once. It is needed to say that the plan at the n units below the second step is the same as the plan at the n units of the first. Without it, the plan at smaller and the plan at belowLarger need not serve the same ends, though both have n units. Then this proof has no way to pass from the first step to the second.

Here too only half of the marginality hypothesis is used: that the end is served at smaller. That it is not served at belowSmaller is not used. The proof names that half _notNeeded. That name is inside the proof, not in the statement. Every hypothesis the statement lists is used.

One result here is not a step in the derivation. The claim (SwapDominant) is made about one plan: the man's rule for which ends he would serve with each set of his horses. It is not made about every plan shaped like it. That rule of method is under The other findings. A claim made of one plan says nothing about other plans. So it does not say that his plan is the only one that satisfies swap dominance. A narrow piece of that is proved instead (no_rival_swap_dominant). It is not uniqueness. It takes asymmetry of preference (AsymmetricPreference): if he prefers one bundle to another, he does not prefer the second to the first. Then no rival plan that is one swap from his, at some set of horses, also satisfies swap dominance. Three earlier parts cite this theorem, so it is shown here: The other findings, The claims and The conditions. The last uses it to show that, given asymmetry, a claim about every plan the man might have can be refuted.

No rival plan, one swap away. Suppose preference is asymmetric. Then two plans over the same stock cannot both be swap-dominant while differing by a single swap at some sub-stock.

Rothbard writes of "the" value scale — "the" marginal unit, "the" least urgent want (MES pp. 24–27) — without arguing there is only one. This is a narrow piece of that: not uniqueness, only that no rival a single swap away at some sub-stock is also swap-dominant. Swap dominance (SwapDominant) plus asymmetry delivers it. Asymmetry is the praxeological claim AsymmetricPreference. This theorem uses it. So does Apodictic.Contrast.rothbard_entails_servedInOrder, shown under Mises's route. The docstring of AsymmetricPreference lists the other theorems that use it. Apart from asymmetry, the man's ranking of bundles (Prefers) has no properties assumed of it.
