1.8. Mises's route🔗

Terms such as library, docstring, signature, claim, condition, linter and frame are explained under The Result.

Rothbard's is not the only derivation the tradition offers. Mises reaches the same law in Human Action (ch. VII, §1). His argument has a wholly different shape. The library carries his route as a second target. It is built in a module that cannot see Rothbard's theorems at all. So whatever his argument spends has to be stated there, and it shows on the signature. This part asks what his argument spends. A proof that could reach for Rothbard's lemmas would have assumed the answer to that question.

His statement of the law:

If the supply available increases from n–1 units to n units, the increment can be employed only for the removal of a want which is less urgent or less painful than the least urgent or least painful among all those wants which could be removed by means of the supply n–1.

This statement is called his ladder here (Mises.LadderHolds). His wording differs from Rothbard's in two ways:

  • His ladder runs upward, from n–1 to n. Rothbard's example runs downward: he takes a horse away, six horses to five (see The horses).

  • His ladder singles out a least urgent want (Mises.LeastUrgentServed). Rothbard's law names no least want. It compares what a fifth horse adds with what a sixth adds (see The theorems).

Mises's statement is read here as Rothbard's is read. Two supplies are held up against each other at one time. The reason is that Mises's own definition has that shape. The marginal employment (Mises.marginalEmployment) is the one a man makes at n units "but would not make if, other things being equal, his supply were only n–1 units". Mises prefaces that definition with a remark: establishing it does not transcend praxeological reasoning. The remark returns at the end of this part. On another reading, the ladder is a history of acquisitions. That reading is a separate target, set out under The ladder as a history.

The docstrings below speak of a few things from the vocabulary (see The vocabulary):

  • A sub-stock is a set of units cut from the man's stock (see The conditions). Each supply compared here is one.

  • The plan (AllocationPlan): for any sub-stock, which wants he would serve with exactly those units. It is settled in advance.

  • The preference (Prefers): his ranking of one bundle of wants above another. A bundle is a set of wants. Nothing more is built into the ranking. The same ranking of single wants is PrefersEnd. One want is more urgent than another when he prefers it in that ranking.

Mises's marginal employment — the ends served at the larger supply and not at the smaller.

"We call that employment of a unit of a homogeneous supply which a man makes if his supply is n units, but would not make if, other things being equal, his supply were only n–1 units, the least urgent employment or the marginal employment, and the utility derived from it marginal utility" (Human Action, ch. VII, §1).

It is stated as the DIFFERENCE of two counterfactual allocations at one time. That is what "other things being equal" asks for.

It restates marginalEnds (in Apodictic.MarginalUtility) deliberately, because Apodictic.Mises does not import Apodictic.MarginalUtility or Apodictic.Urgency. That the two coincide is a theorem for a module importing both, not an import here.

It is a definition, not a claim. Mises introduces it as a definition ("We call that employment ..."). He prefaces it with the remark that establishing it does not transcend praxeological reasoning.

Least urgent among those served — Mises's "the least urgent or least painful among all those wants which could be removed by means of the supply n–1".

It is not a claim about action, and not a condition of any theorem. It is a definition used inside the law's statement. It says of a given end that nothing else served at that sub-stock ranks below it. It is stated of an end the law quantifies over. It is never asserted to exist. Whether such an end exists at all is a separate question. Prefers is not assumed transitive or complete. So the ends served at a sub-stock may have no least one: two of them may be unranked, or three ranked in a cycle. Saying more about Prefers would be needed to rule that out.

Inside the law it enters as a hypothesis on that end, not as a derived object. The law speaks of any end that is least, if there is one. It never constructs one. Mises's sentence names a minimum. Rothbard's derivation never needs one: it compares two named steps. Naming a minimum is the first place the two routes visibly differ.

Mises's ladder — his statement of the law. It is written out as a target, so that what proves it, and from what, is legible.

"If the supply available increases from n–1 units to n units, the increment can be employed only for the removal of a want which is less urgent or less painful than the least urgent or least painful among all those wants which could be removed by means of the supply n–1" (Human Action, ch. VII, §1).

It is a definition of a statement (a def of a Prop), not a theorem, and deliberately so. It says what the law asserts and proves nothing. It is reached twice below, from two different premise sets. ladder_holds derives it from Rothbard's premise. ladder_from_order derives it from Mises's own bridge, ServedInOrder: that the agent serves in order of urgency. Which premises each spends is on each theorem's signature.

His conclusion is Rothbard's. Before asking what Mises's argument spends, ask whether it has to spend anything. Rothbard's premise is swap dominance (SwapDominant, under The claims). Take any sub-stock. Take one want the plan would serve with that sub-stock, and one the good can serve that the plan would leave unserved. Swap the second in for the first. Then the man prefers what the plan would serve to what the swap leaves him. Whether Rothbard may assume this at all is the warrant problem, set out under The finding. Add independence of uses (IndependentUses, under The conditions). Does the ladder (Mises.LadderHolds) follow outright from these two? It does. So his conclusion needs nothing beyond Rothbard's premise and independence of uses. The docstrings below call this check the control. It comes back positive: the ladder follows. The proof is a few lines. Two things fall out of it:

  • The upward direction is narration. The ladder's statement holds two supplies, fewer and more, at one time. Nothing in it runs upward or downward.

  • The least-urgent qualifier does no work at all. The proof consumes only that the want was served at the smaller supply. The machine can show as much, because the same conclusion goes through for every want served there, not only the least urgent.

His sentence singles out "the least urgent or least painful among all those wants which could be removed by means of the supply n–1". This theorem drops the qualifier and keeps the conclusion. Whatever the extra unit adds is less urgent than ANYTHING the smaller supply serves.

Mises's ladder, derived from Rothbard's premise and independence of uses — the control comes back positive.

LadderHolds follows outright from SwapDominant and IndependentUses. Mises's own derivation is the dilemma about intermediate stages and the appeal to the category of action, set out below. This theorem takes no premise from that derivation. So, once the conclusion is read counterfactually (as LadderHolds: two supplies at one time), that derivation supplies nothing the conclusion needs. Read as a history, the ladder is a different statement, with its own theorem and premises (Apodictic.Temporal).

And his minimality qualifier does no work. The hypothesis LeastUrgentServed plan fewer least is a conjunction. Only its first half is consumed: that least is served at the smaller supply. The second half says that nothing served there ranks below it. The proof splits the hypothesis into its two halves and names the second _minimality. The _ marks a name the proof never uses. The linter cannot see this. It judges each hypothesis whole, and both halves arrive in one hypothesis. ladder_for_every_served above is what makes the idleness a machine fact instead of a comment.

So Mises's sentence adds two things to Rothbard's. One is the upward direction. The other is the "least urgent" restriction. The first is a narration, and the second is idle.

The marginal employment (Mises.marginalEmployment), meanwhile, is Rothbard's marginal ends (marginalEnds) under another name. It is the same set, by definition. So there is one law on the table, not two. What differs between the authors is only the argument for it.

The two definitions of the marginal ends coincide. One is Mises's "marginal employment" (Mises.marginalEmployment). It is the employment a man makes at n units and would not make at n–1. The other is Rothbard's marginal utility of a supply (marginalEnds). It is the set of ends the larger stock serves and the smaller does not. The two are the same set, definitionally. Each module states its own, because Apodictic.Mises is isolated. That they agree is proved here, where both are in scope. The agreement is what licenses saying the two routes reach the same law.

His argument is a dilemma. Mises does not assert an ordering premise the way Rothbard does. He holds that the law "is already implied in the category of action". He argues it so:

There are only two alternatives. Either there are or there are not intermediate stages between the felt uneasiness which impels a man to act and the state in which there can no longer be any action … In the second case there could be only one action; as soon as this action is consummated, a state would be reached in which no further action is possible. This is manifestly incompatible with our assumption that there is action; this case no longer implies the general conditions presupposed in the category of action. Only the first case remains.

The dilemma is encoded as it stands. The second horn is read through its stated consequence: there could be only one action (Mises.AtMostOneAction). It is not read through an invented measure of how much uneasiness remains. That keeps the vocabulary as it was. Two small frames then check the argument's two steps, one frame for each step.

First, the refutation of the second horn. Mises says one action followed by a terminal state is incompatible with "our assumption that there is action". It is not. Take a frame that admits exactly one action (Model.Solitary). Both of these hold in it:

  • There is action (Mises.ActionOccurs).

  • There is at most one action (Mises.AtMostOneAction). That is the second horn.

So the two are not incompatible. What his step needs is that action is plural: that two distinct actions occur (Mises.ActionRecurs). The bare statement that there is action (Mises.ActionOccurs) does not deliver plurality. That statement is what the tradition calls the axiom of action, in the only form the tradition states it: there is action. The library names it as a definition and asserts it nowhere. This is the first time the library has put it to any work. It was too weak for the job.

Existence does not refute the second horn. Mises calls one action followed by a terminal state "manifestly incompatible with our assumption that there is action". The frame Solitary admits exactly one action. So both hold in it: there is action (Mises.ActionOccurs), and there is at most one action (Mises.AtMostOneAction), the second horn. So the step is not valid as stated.

What his argument needs is Mises.ActionRecurs: that two distinct actions occur. Nothing in "there is action" delivers it. His own wording gestures past bare existence ("this case no longer implies the general conditions presupposed in the category of action"). It does not say what those conditions are. Those conditions, left unnamed, are the suppressed premise of his derivation.

Second, grant him the horn. Let action be plural, so the dilemma is won. The ladder still does not follow. Take a frame with three features:

  • It has two actions. So action is plural, and that is everything the dilemma establishes.

  • Its preference is asymmetric and not empty. Asymmetric means: if he prefers one bundle of wants to another, he does not prefer the second to the first (AsymmetricPreference, under The claims). The frame satisfies that claim too. Not empty means some want really is preferred to another. With nothing preferred, every ranking the ladder asks for would fail at once, and the failure would show nothing.

  • With no units, its plan would serve the less urgent want. Only with one unit would it serve the more urgent one too. That is what breaks the law.

That frame meets everything the dilemma establishes. And it breaks the law.

Refuting the second horn does not deliver the ladder. Take a frame where action is plural. Mises's dilemma is won outright there. In that frame the ladder fails.

And it fails for a substantive reason, not a degenerate one. Preference here is asymmetric (plural_asymmetric) and non-empty (plural_prefers_nontrivial). What goes wrong is the agent's plan. With no units it would serve the less urgent end. So the one unit brings in something MORE urgent than what the smaller supply would serve. Mises's sentence is false of this agent. Everything the dilemma establishes is true of him.

So the step from "there are intermediate stages" to the law is not valid on its own. What has to be added is that the plan respects the ordering. The library reconstructs it as ServedInOrder. That is strictly weaker than swap dominance (SwapDominant) taken with independence of uses and asymmetry. Those three entail it (Apodictic.Contrast.rothbard_entails_servedInOrder). It does not entail swap dominance (servedInOrder_not_entail_swapDominant, below). In Mises's own text the addition is a single unargued sentence: "It is nothing else than the reverse of the statement that what satisfies more is preferred to what gives smaller satisfaction."

This does not rule Mises's derivation out. It shows that the dilemma is not the premise the law needs.

So the dilemma does not supply the premise the law needs. What carries Mises from it to the law is a single sentence. In his text it comes straight after this one: "Thus the law of marginal utility is already implied in the category of action." He offers it as though it added nothing: "It is nothing else than the reverse of the statement that what satisfies more is preferred to what gives smaller satisfaction." That second sentence is where his premise for the law lives. It is reconstructed below as ServedInOrder. The sentence is unargued.

His premise, reconstructed. There is a tempting reading of that sentence. It adds a scale of satisfaction to the vocabulary. The scale would be a second ordering, on states, by how much uneasiness each removes. That reading was refused, for three reasons:

  • It would enlarge the ontology on one sentence's authority.

  • It invites cardinal degrees through the back door: how much one state satisfies, as an amount. The ranking here attaches no numbers (see The horses).

  • A satisfaction scale would still have to be connected to which want a unit serves. That connection is the premise all over again.

The reading used instead is the most generous one the existing vocabulary allows. The agent serves in order of urgency (ServedInOrder). Take any sub-stock on hand. Suppose the good can serve a want, and that want is more urgent than one the plan would serve with that sub-stock. Then the plan would serve it too, with the same sub-stock. That is a praxeological claim. Like Rothbard's, it is subjunctive throughout. It is the second under The claims.

One thing more is needed. Serving in order shows only this: the want a unit adds is not more urgent than any want already served at the smaller supply. The law says it is less urgent. To pass from the one to the other, the two wants must be ranked one way or the other. The route takes this as a condition, not a claim. The condition says that every pair of wants the good can serve is ranked, one way or the other (stock.ComparableServiceable). Where a pair is not, the theorem is silent. This condition is the fourth under The conditions. It is the one Rothbard's route never asks for.

The two premise sets, related. Are the routes independent, then? Not quite. The relation runs one way. Grant three things:

  • Rothbard's premise (SwapDominant).

  • Independence of uses (IndependentUses agent time).

  • Asymmetry of preference (AsymmetricPreference).

Swap dominance, independence of uses and asymmetry entail Mises's bridge.

Grant swap dominance (SwapDominant), independence of uses (IndependentUses agent time) and asymmetry of preference (AsymmetricPreference). Then the agent serves in order of urgency. That is exactly ServedInOrder, the premise the Misesian route was built on.

So the two routes are NOT independent. Whatever else is true of Mises's derivation, its premise is available to anyone who already grants these three. The second route's bridge (ServedInOrder) therefore adds no claim beyond them. Mises's route also carries a condition, comparability (ComparableServiceable), which is not among them.

The proof is the contrapositive of served_over_unserved. Suppose the more urgent end were left unserved. Then Rothbard's workhorse, served_over_unserved, says the served end is preferred to it. And asymmetry contradicts the assumption that it was the more urgent of the two. The proof splits on whether the end is served. That split needs no classical logic (Classical.choice). plan.wouldServe subStock is a finite set (a Finset). Whether an end belongs to a finite set can be decided once any two ends can be told apart ([DecidableEq praxis.End]). This theorem carries that, as Rothbard's route does.

The converse fails. Serving in order (ServedInOrder) compares single wants. Swap dominance (SwapDominant) compares bundles: what the plan would serve, against the same bundle with one swap made. A frame can rank wants and serve them in order. It can still be too coarse to rank those bundles. Then serving in order holds where swap dominance does not.

So on the allocation, Mises's premise is strictly weaker than swap dominance taken with independence of uses and asymmetry. Still, neither premise set contains the other. The reason is that his route pays for the weaker premise with comparability of wants (stock.ComparableServiceable). Rothbard's route never needs it. Mises asks less of the agent's plan and more of the agent's value scale. Comparability is a condition, not a claim. So the price is paid in situations: where comparability fails, Mises's route is silent. There are agents for whom Rothbard's premise holds and Mises's route does not apply. The gap is narrow. Why it is narrow is itself a small finding. Swap dominance, with independence of uses (IndependentUses), already ranks each served want against each unserved one the good could reach (served_over_unserved, under The theorems). So every pair that some sub-stock splits, one want served there and the other not, is already ranked. Comparability can then fail only for a pair that no sub-stock splits: at every sub-stock, the plan serves both or neither. That is why the gap is narrow.

Comparability is a condition. So this is what "Mises's law covers fewer situations" means concretely. It means an agent with two ends he has never had occasion to rank against each other. Suppose comparability were a claim. Then praxeology would assert that no such agent exists. This frame would then be a counter-example to the claim, not a gap in the theorem's reach.

Where this leaves the question. The finding left one question. May praxeology help itself to a standing plan? A standing plan is settled in advance. It answers for every set of the man's units at once, and no act of his settles it. Rothbard's rule is that "value scales do not exist in a void apart from the concrete choices of action" (p. 33). The plan is a scale of the kind that rule denies. That is the case made under The finding.

Mises's route does not escape that question. It meets it in another form. His premise is as counterfactual as Rothbard's. It says what the agent would serve at every smaller supply. And Mises restricts value scales exactly as Rothbard does, three chapters earlier: "These scales have no independent existence apart from the actual behavior of individuals" (ch. IV, §2). So the warrant problem is Mises's too.

The two authors meet it differently:

  • Rothbard exempts the law by name, on the page that states his rule. No one can predict with certainty the course of a man's choices, he says, "except that they will follow the law of marginal utility, which was deduced from the axiom of action". That clause is what lets him hand the man a standing plan. The law is then proved from that plan. So his warrant is circular (see The finding).

  • Mises instead simply announces that the counterfactual costs nothing: "We do not transcend the sphere of praxeological reasoning in establishing the following definition". The definition is the marginal employment, quoted near the top of this part. It is counterfactual: the employment he makes at n units but would not make if, other things being equal, his supply were only n–1 units.

Neither warrants the premise. That is as far as this part goes.
