1.8. Mises's route🔗

Rothbard's is not the only derivation the tradition offers. Mises reaches the same law in Human Action (ch. VII, §1). His argument has a wholly different shape. The library carries his route as a second target. It is built in a module that cannot see Rothbard's theorems at all. So whatever his argument spends has to be stated there, and it shows on the signature. This part asks what his argument spends. A proof that could reach for Rothbard's lemmas would have assumed the answer.

His statement of the law:

If the supply available increases from n–1 units to n units, the increment can be employed only for the removal of a want which is less urgent or less painful than the least urgent or least painful among all those wants which could be removed by means of the supply n–1.

His wording differs from Rothbard's in two ways:

  • His ladder runs upward, from n–1 to n. Rothbard takes a horse away.

  • His ladder singles out a least urgent want (Mises.LeastUrgentServed). Rothbard compares two named steps.

Mises's statement is read here as Rothbard's is read. Two supplies are held up against each other at one time. The reason is that Mises's own definition has that shape. The marginal employment (Mises.marginalEmployment) is the one a man makes at n units "but would not make if, other things being equal, his supply were only n–1 units". On another reading, the ladder is a history of acquisitions. That reading is a separate target, set out under The ladder as a history.

Mises's marginal employment — the ends served at the larger supply and not at the smaller.

"We call that employment of a unit of a homogeneous supply which a man makes if his supply is n units, but would not make if, other things being equal, his supply were only n–1 units, the least urgent employment or the marginal employment, and the utility derived from it marginal utility" (Human Action, ch. VII, §1).

It is stated as the DIFFERENCE of two counterfactual allocations at one time. That is what "other things being equal" asks for.

It restates marginalEnds (in Apodictic.MarginalUtility) deliberately, because Apodictic.Mises does not import Apodictic.MarginalUtility or Apodictic.Urgency. That the two coincide is a theorem for a module importing both, not an import here.

It is a definition, not a claim. Mises introduces it as a definition ("We call that employment ..."). He prefaces it with the remark that establishing it does not transcend praxeological reasoning.

His conclusion is Rothbard's. Before asking what Mises's argument spends, ask whether it has to spend anything. Mises's ladder, derived from Rothbard's premise and independence of uses — the control comes back positive.

LadderHolds follows outright from SwapDominant and IndependentUses. Mises's own derivation is the dilemma about intermediate stages and the appeal to the category of action. Once the conclusion is read counterfactually, that derivation supplies nothing the conclusion needs.

And his minimality qualifier does no work. The hypothesis LeastUrgentServed plan fewer least is a conjunction. Only its first half is consumed: that least is served at the smaller supply. The second half says that nothing served there ranks below it. That half is destructured as _minimality and never used. The linter cannot see this, because it works a whole binder at a time. Both halves arrive in one binder. ladder_for_every_served above is what makes the idleness a machine fact instead of a comment.

So Mises's sentence adds two things to Rothbard's. One is the upward direction. The other is the "least urgent" restriction. The first is a narration, and the second is idle.

The marginal employment (Mises.marginalEmployment), meanwhile, is Rothbard's marginal ends (marginalEnds) under another name. It is the same set, by definition. So there is one law on the table, not two. What differs between the authors is only the argument for it.

The two definitions of the marginal ends coincide. One is Mises's "marginal employment" (Mises.marginalEmployment). It is the employment a man makes at n units and would not make at n–1. The other is Rothbard's marginal utility of a supply (marginalEnds). It is the set of ends the larger stock serves and the smaller does not. The two are the same set, definitionally. Each module states its own, because Apodictic.Mises is isolated. That they agree is proved here, where both are in scope. The agreement is what licenses saying the two routes reach the same law.

His argument is a dilemma. Mises does not assert an ordering premise the way Rothbard does. He holds that the law "is already implied in the category of action". He argues it so:

There are only two alternatives. Either there are or there are not intermediate stages between the felt uneasiness which impels a man to act and the state in which there can no longer be any action … In the second case there could be only one action; as soon as this action is consummated, a state would be reached in which no further action is possible. This is manifestly incompatible with our assumption that there is action; this case no longer implies the general conditions presupposed in the category of action. Only the first case remains.

The dilemma is encoded as it stands. The second horn is read through its stated consequence: there could be only one action (Mises.AtMostOneAction). It is not read through an invented measure of how much uneasiness remains. That keeps the vocabulary as it was. Two small frames then check the argument's two steps, one frame for each step.

First, the refutation of the second horn. Mises says one action followed by a terminal state is incompatible with "our assumption that there is action". It is not. Take a frame that admits exactly one action. It satisfies both halves he calls incompatible. What his step needs is that action is plural: that two distinct actions occur (Mises.ActionRecurs). The bare claim that there is action (Mises.ActionOccurs) does not deliver plurality. This is the first time the library has put that claim to any work. It was too weak for the job.

Existence does not refute the second horn. Mises calls one action followed by a terminal state "manifestly incompatible with our assumption that there is action". The frame Solitary admits exactly one action. It satisfies both halves he calls incompatible. So the step is not valid as stated.

What his argument needs is Mises.ActionRecurs: that two distinct actions occur. Nothing in "there is action" delivers it. His own wording gestures past bare existence ("this case no longer implies the general conditions presupposed in the category of action"). It does not say what those conditions are. Naming them is the suppressed premise of his derivation.

Second, grant him the horn. Let action be plural, so the dilemma is won. The ladder still does not follow. Take a frame with three features:

  • It has two actions.

  • Its preference is asymmetric and not empty.

  • Its plan serves the less urgent want first.

That frame meets everything the dilemma establishes. And it breaks the law.

And it fails for a substantive reason, not a degenerate one. Preference here is asymmetric (plural_asymmetric) and non-empty (plural_prefers_nontrivial). What goes wrong is the agent's plan. It serves the less urgent end first. So the unit he adds brings in something MORE urgent than what he was already serving. Mises's sentence is false of this agent. Everything the dilemma establishes is true of him.

So the step from "there are intermediate stages" to the law is not valid on its own. What has to be added is that the plan respects the ordering. The library reconstructs it as ServedInOrder. That is strictly weaker than Rothbard's premises (SwapDominant, with independence of uses and asymmetry). In Mises's own text the addition is a single unargued sentence: "It is nothing else than the reverse of the statement that what satisfies more is preferred to what gives smaller satisfaction."

This does not show Mises's derivation is unsalvageable. It shows the dilemma is not the premise.

So the dilemma is not the premise. What carries Mises from it to the law is a single sentence. He offers it as though it added nothing: "It is nothing else than the reverse of the statement that what satisfies more is preferred to what gives smaller satisfaction." That sentence is where his premise lives. The sentence is unargued.

His premise, reconstructed. There is a tempting reading of that sentence. It adds a scale of satisfaction to the vocabulary. The scale would be a second ordering, on states, by how much uneasiness each removes. That reading was refused, for three reasons:

  • It would enlarge the ontology on one sentence's authority.

  • It invites cardinal degrees through the back door.

  • A satisfaction scale would still have to be connected to which want a unit serves. That connection is the premise all over again.

The reading used instead is the most generous one the existing vocabulary allows. The agent serves in order of urgency (ServedInOrder). Suppose the good can serve a want, and that want is more urgent than one on his plan. Then the plan has it too. That is a praxeological claim. Like Rothbard's, it is subjunctive throughout. It is the second under The claims.

One thing more is needed. It is a condition, not a claim. It says that every pair of wants the good can serve is ranked, one way or the other (stock.ComparableServiceable). Where a pair is not, the theorem is silent. This condition is the fourth under The conditions. It is the one Rothbard's route never asks for.

Rothbard's premises entail Mises's bridge.

Grant swap dominance (SwapDominant), independence of uses (IndependentUses agent time) and asymmetry of preference (AsymmetricPreference). Then the agent serves in order of urgency. That is exactly ServedInOrder, the premise the Misesian route was built on.

So the two routes are NOT independent. Whatever else is true of Mises's derivation, its premise is available to anyone who already has Rothbard's. The second route's bridge (ServedInOrder) therefore adds no premise the first route lacked.

The proof is the contrapositive of served_over_unserved. Suppose the more urgent end were left unserved. Then Rothbard's workhorse, served_over_unserved, says the served end is preferred to it. Comparability is a condition. So this is what "Mises's law covers fewer situations" means concretely. It means an agent with two ends he has never had occasion to rank against each other. Suppose comparability were a claim. Then praxeology would assert that no such agent exists. This frame would then be a counter-example to the claim, not a gap in the theorem's reach.

Where this leaves the question. The finding left one question. May praxeology help itself to a standing plan? Mises's route does not escape that question. It meets it in another form. His premise is as counterfactual as Rothbard's. It says what the agent would serve at every smaller supply. And Mises restricts value scales exactly as Rothbard does, three chapters earlier: "These scales have no independent existence apart from the actual behavior of individuals" (ch. IV, §2). Rothbard exempts the law by name. Mises instead simply announces that the counterfactual costs nothing: "We do not transcend the sphere of praxeological reasoning in establishing the following definition". Neither warrants the premise. That is as far as this part goes.
