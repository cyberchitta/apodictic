1.14. Interest🔗

In brief. Rothbard concludes that a pure rate of interest can never be zero or negative. From 2014, banks in the euro area lent to one another overnight at a negative rate. This part rebuilds his argument. It asks which premise such a lender fails.

The argument has four steps, S2 to S5. Rothbard gives an example that fits them. John Smith holds money in units of ten ounces. He weighs each unit against money ten years from now (MES, p. 380). On his scale, ten ounces in ten years rank below every unit he holds now.

Rothbard never says what Smith would buy. Some conditions depend on it. So we give him something to buy today: a coat. The coat is ours, not Rothbard's.

Take one unit of ten ounces. Three sums in ten years matter:

  • The promise. It would buy then what ten ounces buy now. If prices stay the same, it is ten ounces.

  • A smaller sum, say nine ounces.

  • The repayment. This is what a loan actually pays back.

The lists below leave out bookkeeping, such as which stock a sum comes from.

S2: present money beats the same amount of future money. Smith prefers ten ounces today to the promise. This uses the claim of time preference (TimePreference): he prefers a satisfaction sooner to the same satisfaction later. Three conditions make it the same satisfaction, and sooner:

  • The promised money would buy a coat as good as today's. (SamePurchasingPower. Rising prices break it.)

  • He counts the promise as the money it promises. (PromisePays. Doubt about the borrower breaks it.)

  • He would spend today's ounces first. (SpentSooner. It breaks if he would keep them unspent until the promise pays.)

S3: …and beats any smaller amount. Smith also prefers ten ounces today to nine in ten years. This uses a second claim: more money is preferred to less (MoreMoneyPreferredWhenSpent). The ranking is made when he spends. It covers only sums that buy different things. Six conditions go with it:

  • Any smaller part of the promise buys something different from the whole. (BuysMoreThanItsParts.)

  • If he will rank the promise above nine when he spends them, he already ranks it so today. (RankedAlikeNowOf. The impulse man breaks it. Today he would rather not have the cash he will spend on an impulse tomorrow.)

  • The promise is of money, not of other goods. (MoneyFrame.Money.)

  • Holding the promise, or any part of it, costs him nothing. (FreeToHoldWithin.)

  • Any nine future ounces are as good as any other nine. (Homogeneous.)

  • His preferences are transitive across the three sums (TransitiveOn). Ten today beats the promise. The promise beats nine. So ten today beats nine.

S4: so he does not lend at zero or below. Suppose he lent ten ounces and got back ten, or nine. The loan would not show that he preferred being repaid to keeping his ten ounces. This uses a third claim, asymmetry (AsymmetricPreference): he cannot prefer each of two things to the other. Three conditions:

  • Had he said no, he would have kept his ten ounces at full value. (RefusalKeepsSum.)

  • After the loan, what he holds is worth what the repayment buys, and no more. (Trade.AfterCountsAsPlanned.)

  • The promise is as many ounces as he lent. (SameCount.)

S5: so every rate struck is positive. This uses the fourth claim, demonstrated preference (DemonstratedPreference). It is asserted of this one loan. Lending showed a preference. Smith preferred his holding after the loan to what refusing would have left him. Together with S4, this means a loan he makes repays more than ten.

Rothbard's scale says more. Smith's first unit ranks above eleven future ounces and below twelve. So he would lend it for twelve, but not for eleven.

S5 does not assume that saying no keeps his ten ounces at full value. It gets this from two conditions:

  • The loan was voluntary. Saying no would not have cost him anything. (Trade.Voluntary.)

  • Before the loan, what he held was worth what ten ounces buy: the coat. (Trade.BeforeCountsAsPlanned.)

The condition on his holding before, and S4's second one, ask something of Smith. He holds other units of ten ounces. Those, and anything else he keeps, must add nothing of their own.

Some apparent premises do no work, in some steps or in all:

  • At a zero rate, the second claim. Only a negative rate needs "more money is preferred". The zero-rate theorem also drops five of that claim's six conditions. Of S3's conditions, the theorem for a zero rate keeps only interchangeable units.

  • "The same amount", in S2 and S3. Neither step ties the promise's ounces to the present sum. S2 counts nothing. S3 counts only the smaller sum against the promise. So S3 compares nine with the promise, not with today's ten. Only the loan theorems tie the promise's ounces to the sum lent, with a condition of its own (SameCount).

  • That holding the present sum costs nothing. No step assumes it. A cost of holding it reaches the loan only through refusal. (That the promise is free to hold is one of S3's conditions.)

  • The borrower, and the market. Every step is about one lender.

  • Any property of the order of time, and any classical step.

What each case breaks:

  • The bank that lent overnight at a negative rate. It breaks one of two conditions. If it had no use for the money that day, it breaks SpentSooner. Or it breaks RefusalKeepsSum: a bank that refused kept the sum at a cost. It held reserves the central bank charged for, or banknotes that had to be guarded. In S5, refusal is derived from two conditions. The same cost breaks one of them: the loan being voluntary (Trade.Voluntary), or the holding before it counting as planned (Trade.BeforeCountsAsPlanned). This is a reading of the case. No frame is built for it.

  • The Christmas club saver. He lends at zero, and he breaks SamePurchasingPower. His cash would buy an impulse. The club pays for what he saves for. Built in the library.

  • The impulse man. Today he would rather not hold the cash he knows he will spend on an impulse tomorrow. He breaks RankedAlikeNowOf (S3), comparing his cash with no cash. He is never put in a loan. Built in the library.

  • Rising prices, and doubt about the borrower. Rising prices break SamePurchasingPower. Doubt breaks PromisePays. Both are S2's conditions. Each can break while the other holds. Built in the library.

The detail follows.

Interest is the fifth target. Time preference was built for it. Rothbard's conclusion is categorical: "every man’s time preference is positive, i.e., one ounce of present money will always be preferred to one ounce or less of future money. Therefore, there will never be any question of a zero or negative pure interest rate" (MES, p. 382). It was chosen because the world seems to have refuted that conclusion. From 2014, banks in the euro area lent to one another overnight at a negative rate. An overnight loan is repaid the next day. It cannot be sold on for a gain before then. So the lender knows, when he lends, that he will be repaid less. The question is which step of the argument such a lender falls outside.

Rothbard's argument has four steps, rebuilt here in order:

  • Present money is preferred to the same amount of future money.

  • So it is preferred to that amount or less.

  • So a man does not lend at a rate of zero or below.

  • So every rate actually struck is positive.

The docstrings below label them S2 to S5. The rate as a market number is not reached. Nothing here adds up lenders or finds an equilibrium.

Money. The vocabulary gets two more relations. One says which units are money. The other says whether holding a sum costs anything. Nothing else is added.

Money m: the unit m is an amount of money, or a promise of an amount of money at a later date. Money is Mises's: "the thing which serves as the generally accepted and commonly used medium of exchange" (Human Action, ch. XVII, §3). Supplied; no properties: whether a unit is money is a fact about the situation, checked by whoever applies a theorem, and each theorem that needs it names it as a hypothesis.

FreeToHold : self.Agent → self.Time → Finset self.Means → Prop

FreeToHold agent t sum: at t, holding exactly the units in sum costs the agent nothing — no storage, custody, tax or risk of loss eats into it. Supplied; no properties.

The man has a plan for his money. A sum counts for what the plan buys with exactly those units. Goods in a trade are counted differently, under Exchange. There a holding counts for every end any of its goods could serve. For money the two counts differ at once. An ounce can be spent now or kept for later, but not both.

Three stocks are weighed at the date of the loan:

  • the present money;

  • the promises of future money;

  • the money promised, as he would spend it if he had it in hand.

A theorem takes a sum from each.

Present money over future money. Rothbard: "It follows from the law of time preference that present money is worth more than present expectations of the same amount of future money" (MES, p. 376). It does follow, but not from the amount.

"The same amount" does no work here, and no count is carried: what the sums would buy does (SamePurchasingPower), and that the promise pays (PromisePays). The count enters with the loans (SameCount). And the conclusion needs the present sum to be spent sooner — the case Rothbard's n. 11 sets aside.

Same purchasing power — a condition on the situation. Each of two sums would buy one satisfaction, the same satisfaction, each at its own date. Asserted in the theorems between the present sum and the money promised.

Mises: "present goods are valued higher than future goods of the same kind and quantity" (Human Action, ch. XIX, §1). Rothbard's "the same amount of future money" (MES p. 376) is not part of it: equal counts of ounces are the same good at two dates only if they buy the same thing, and that is what this says. The count is the separate condition SameCount, carried only where it is used. It fails where the money buys less at the later date (Rothbard's purchasing-power component, MES p. 797; Mises's price premium). Whether the promise pays is the separate condition PromisePays.

Spent sooner — a condition on the situation. The present sum would be spent before the promise pays, and not on something already past at now.

It is what Rothbard's footnote answers rather than grants: "It is not valid to object that some might prefer to use the money in the future rather than in the present. That is not the issue here, which is one of availability for use" (MES p. 386 n. 11). The objector is the man for whom this condition fails — he would hold the present sum until the date the promise pays.

This step has three findings.

The number of ounces does no work. Equal counts are the same good at two dates only if they buy the same thing. That is what the proof uses. So none of the conditions above says the sums are the same amount. The count enters later. There "repaid no more than lent" has to be compared with a promise. The count is a condition of its own (SameCount). (SamePurchasingPower and PromisePays once each held an equality of counts that this step never used. The linter checks whole assumptions, not their parts. So it could not see that. The counts were moved out by hand.)

Two things a reader might merge are kept apart. One is that the money will buy as much later. Rising prices break it. The other is that the promise will pay. A risk of default breaks it. Each has its own name, so a failure points at one of them. A frame shows this. It has one agent, with an ounce and a promise of one. Each condition fails while the other holds. A single condition, stated between the cash and the promise, fails the same way in both cases. So it cannot say which happened:

Holding. What the step needs is that the present sum would be spent sooner. That holding it costs nothing is in no signature. No step uses it. A theorem that listed it would fail the linter. (A cost of holding cash does reach the loan theorems, by another road. It enters through the condition that refusing a loan leaves the sum at full value, below.)

Rothbard's footnote dismisses the man who would keep the money for later, as "not the issue here, which is one of availability for use" (MES, p. 386 n. 11). Suppose that man would spend the present sum on the same thing, at the same date, as the promise. Then the two sums buy the same bundle. Asymmetry then forbids a strict preference either way. At best his two sums rank equal. The footnote gives him no strict preference.

The same count — a condition on the situation, of bookkeeping: the promise is as many units as the present sum. Rothbard's "the same amount of future money" (MES p. 376), where it does work: only the loan theorems use it, to compare the repaid sum with the promise.

"More money is preferred" is the claim the next step needs. It is spent on one thing only: ruling out a negative rate.

One ounce or less. "Or less" needs a second claim. Rothbard: "[T]o both present money and future money there applies the general rule that more of a good will have greater utility than less of it" (MES, p. 379). It is the tenth claim under The claims. Its docstring there gives the reasons it is narrower than Rothbard's rule. Two of them are findings of the build.

The first is satiation. Read as Rothbard states it, the rule ranks the larger sum above the smaller, whatever each would buy. That holds even when the rule is cut to money free to hold, and to sums one part of the other:

The unrestricted rule says money never satiates. With asymmetry, it gives BuysMoreThanItsParts of every free sum of money: were a strict part to buy what the whole buys, the rule would have the agent prefer one bundle to itself, which asymmetry forbids. So the rule as stated asserts, unseen, that every extra unit of free money buys something more. The library's claim ranks only purchases that differ, and the theorems that need non-satiation carry it as a condition.

Mises denies that money never satiates, for the cash a man holds: "He who says that his thirst for more money can never be quenched, does not mean to say that his cash holding can never be too large. What he really means is that he can never be rich enough" (Human Action, ch. XVII, §3). Rothbard agrees that "effective demand for money is not and cannot be unlimited" (MES, p. 773). So the claim here ranks only sums that buy different things. A condition says that every part of the larger sum buys something other than the whole (BuysMoreThanItsParts):

  • the promise's parts are free to hold;

  • his preferences are transitive across the three sums compared;

  logical background: [propext, Quot.sound]

Four claims carry it: time preference, "more money is preferred", asymmetry, and demonstrated preference. The last is stated of the lender's trade. The borrower contributes nothing. The result about rates struck is about each lender. It says nothing about a market. No borrower who would pay a positive rate is shown to exist.

What the order of time was asked for. Nothing, as under Time preference. No proof here uses a property of the relation "before". None needs a classical step. The one case split is on the size of a sum.

The case. The library builds one frame to test this on. A lender has a use for an ounce now. He lends it to be repaid two at the next date. Every claim and condition of the last theorem holds.

Non-vacuity: every rate struck is positive, applied. The lender lends one ounce and is repaid two; every claim and every condition of S5 hold, with the promised money its own stock.

The test is thin, and here is where:

  • The lender's ranking does not change with the date. So RankedAlikeNowOf holds trivially.

  • The money promised is the promises themselves. So PromisePays is never tried on a promise that might not pay.

  • Every unit counts as money, and every sum is free to hold. So neither of those conditions is tried (MoneyFrame.Money, FreeToHoldWithin).

  • Preference is transitive throughout the frame, not only across the three sums.

  • The promise is one unit. So BuysMoreThanItsParts compares it only with no units.

  • There is one agent, lender and borrower both.

What a failure would point at. Suppose a man lends and is repaid less. The theorem for a rate not above zero then says one of two things. Either his trade did not demonstrate a preference for being repaid over keeping the sum, or one of the theorem's assumptions failed. These are the ones a case is likely to land on:

  • The money would buy less at repayment (SamePurchasingPower).

  • He doubted the promise would pay (PromisePays).

  • He had no use for the sum before the promise paid (SpentSooner).

  • Refusing would not have left him the sum at full value, because keeping cash cost him something (RefusalKeepsSum). (In the last theorem this condition is derived, not assumed. It comes from the trade being voluntary and his holding counting as planned. So there the failure lands on one of those.)

  • An extra unit of the promise bought nothing more (BuysMoreThanItsParts).

  • He ranked the promise against the repayment differently now than at the spending (RankedAlikeNowOf).

  • The promise was not of money, or not free to hold (MoneyFrame.Money, FreeToHoldWithin).

  • His preferences were not transitive across the three sums (TransitiveOn).

  • One of the claims failed.

That theorem's manifest has a few more that a case is less likely to land on. The promise and the repayment come from the stock of promises. The promise is as many units as the sum lent (SameCount). The future units are interchangeable (Homogeneous). What he holds after counts as planned (Trade.AfterCountsAsPlanned).

The bank that lends overnight at a negative rate is likely to land on two of these. One is SpentSooner. The bank may have had no use for the money before repayment, a day later. Then the theorem says nothing more about the bank. The other is RefusalKeepsSum. Refusing would not have left the bank the sum at full value. A bank that refused kept the sum as reserves at the central bank, which charged for them. Or it kept banknotes, which have to be guarded. Either failure takes the bank outside the theorem. Neither is shown here to be the only way out.

Rothbard's own terms give an Austrian a reply to part of this. He never applied it to negative rates. A deposit of present money is like goods left in a warehouse. Redeeming it "is not the completion of a transaction or equivalent to a creditor’s calling his loan; it is a simple repossessing of a man’s own good" (MES, p. 800). The man who deposits goods "pays the owner of the warehouse a certain sum for the service of storage. He still retains ownership of the property; the owner of the warehouse is simply guarding it" (MES, p. 801). On that reading, a negative rate on a deposit is a fee for safekeeping. It is not interest at all. The theorems never applied to it.

The reply does not reach the loan between two banks. That loan is a promise of money tomorrow: a credit transaction, on Rothbard's own terms. But the fee does reach the lender's refusal. It is what keeping the sum would have cost. For banknotes the cost is plainly one of keeping the sum safe. It is a price for guarding, not for time.

For reserves, it turns on what the central bank's overnight deposit is. If it is a warehouse, its charge is a fee for safekeeping. If it is a loan, its charge is a negative rate in its own right. That is left open here. Nothing rests on it, for this reason. From June 2014 the same charge also fell on a bank's excess reserves. These are the reserves it held above the required minimum, in its ordinary account at the central bank. Those reserves do not stand for money stored elsewhere. They are the money itself, as banknotes are. So a bank that refused the overnight loan had nowhere to keep the sum for nothing. All of this is a reading of the case, not a frame built for it.

Where a man lends at exactly zero, the list is shorter. It drops the claim that more money is preferred, and the five conditions that come with it.

The Christmas club saver lends at zero. A sum counts for what he would buy with it. So he is outside the theorem at once. The cash in his hand would buy what he would do with it on impulse. The club pays for what he saves it for. Those are not the same satisfaction. So SamePurchasingPower fails. It fails between his cash and any money the club's promise pays in.
