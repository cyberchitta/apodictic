1.14. Interest🔗

In brief. Rothbard concludes that a pure rate of interest can never be zero or negative. From 2014, banks in the euro area lent to one another overnight at a negative rate. This part rebuilds his argument and asks which of its premises such a lender does not satisfy.

The argument runs in four steps, S2 to S5, and each adds what it needs:

  • Present money is preferred to the same amount of future money (S2). It needs one claim, time preference, and three conditions: the money will buy the same satisfaction later (SamePurchasingPower), the promise will pay (PromisePays), and the present sum would be spent sooner (SpentSooner).

  • …and to any smaller amount (S3). It needs a second claim, that more money is preferred where it is spent, and six conditions with it: every part of the promise buys something other than the whole (BuysMoreThanItsParts); he ranks the two sums now as he will when he spends them (RankedAlikeNowOf); the promise is money; it is free to hold; its units are interchangeable; and his preferences chain where the step uses them.

  • So no one lends at zero or below (S4). It needs a third claim, asymmetry, and three conditions: refusing the loan would have left him the sum at full value (RefusalKeepsSum), what he holds after counts as planned, and the promise is as many units as the sum lent (SameCount). What it concludes is that such a loan does not demonstrate a preference for being repaid over keeping the sum.

  • So every rate struck is positive (S5). It adds the fourth claim, demonstrated preference. Refusing at full value is then not assumed but derived, from the trade being voluntary and his holding before it counting as planned.

Some things the argument seems to need do no work, at least in part of it:

  • At a rate of exactly zero, the second claim and five of its six conditions: only a negative rate needs them, and the theorem for a zero rate carries only interchangeable units.

  • "The same amount", in S2 and S3: neither ties the number of ounces in the promise to the present sum. S2 counts nothing; S3 counts only the smaller sum against the promise, so its "or less" is measured against what buys the same satisfaction, not against the present sum. The count between the promise and the sum lent is a condition of its own, SameCount, and only the loan theorems carry it.

  • That holding the present sum costs nothing. No step assumes it; a cost of holding it reaches the loan only through refusal. (That the promise is free to hold is one of S3's conditions.)

  • The borrower, and the market: every step is about one lender.

  • Any property of the order of time, and any classical step.

What each case breaks:

  • The bank lending overnight at a negative rate breaks SpentSooner, if it had no use for the money for that day, or RefusalKeepsSum, because a bank that refused kept the sum at a cost: reserves the central bank charged for, or banknotes that had to be guarded. In S5, where refusal is derived, the same cost breaks the trade being voluntary or the holding before it counting as planned. This is a reading of the case, not a frame built for it.

  • The Christmas club saver, who lends at zero, breaks SamePurchasingPower: his cash would buy an impulse, the club pays for what he saves for. Built in the library.

  • The man who would rather not hold, today, the cash he knows he will spend on an impulse tomorrow breaks RankedAlikeNowOf, asked of that cash against none. He is never put in a loan. Built in the library.

  • Rising prices break SamePurchasingPower, and a doubt that the borrower will pay breaks PromisePays, each without the other. Built in the library.

The detail follows.

Interest is the fifth target, and it is the one time preference was built for. Rothbard's conclusion is categorical: "every man’s time preference is positive, i.e., one ounce of present money will always be preferred to one ounce or less of future money. Therefore, there will never be any question of a zero or negative pure interest rate" (MES, p. 382). It was chosen because the world seems to have answered it. From 2014, banks in the euro area lent to one another overnight at a negative rate. An overnight loan is repaid the next day, and it cannot be sold on for a gain before then, so the lender knows when he lends that he will be repaid less. The question is which step of the argument such a lender falls outside.

Rothbard's argument runs in four steps, and they are rebuilt here in order. Present money is preferred to the same amount of future money. So it is preferred to that amount or less. So a man does not lend at a rate of zero or below. So every rate actually struck is positive. The docstrings below label them S2 to S5. The rate as a number set in a market is not reached: nothing here adds up lenders or finds an equilibrium.

Money m: the unit m is an amount of money, or a promise of an amount of money at a later date. Money is Mises's: "the thing which serves as the generally accepted and commonly used medium of exchange" (Human Action, ch. XVII, §3). Supplied; no properties: whether a unit is money is a fact about the situation, checked by whoever applies a theorem, and each theorem that needs it names it as a hypothesis.

FreeToHold : self.Agent → self.Time → Finset self.Means → Prop

FreeToHold agent t sum: at t, holding exactly the units in sum costs the agent nothing — no storage, custody, tax or risk of loss eats into it. Supplied; no properties.

A sum of money counts for what the man's plan says he would buy with exactly those units. That is not how goods in a trade are counted under Exchange, where a holding counts for every end any of its goods could serve. For money the two part at once: an ounce can be spent now or kept for later, not both. Three stocks are weighed at the one date of the loan: the present money, the promises of future money, and the money promised, as he would spend it were it in hand. A theorem takes a sum from each.

"The same amount" does no work here, and no count is carried: what the sums would buy does (SamePurchasingPower), and that the promise pays (PromisePays). The count enters with the loans (SameCount). Same purchasing power — a condition on the situation. Each of two sums would buy one satisfaction, the same satisfaction, each at its own date. Asserted in the theorems between the present sum and the money promised.

Mises: "present goods are valued higher than future goods of the same kind and quantity" (Human Action, ch. XIX, §1). Rothbard's "the same amount of future money" (MES p. 376) is not part of it: equal counts of ounces are the same good at two dates only if they buy the same thing, and that is what this says. The count is the separate condition SameCount, carried only where it is used. It fails where the money buys less at the later date (Rothbard's purchasing-power component, MES p. 797; Mises's price premium). Whether the promise pays is the separate condition PromisePays.

Spent sooner — a condition on the situation. The present sum would be spent before the promise pays, and not on something already past at now.

It is what Rothbard's footnote answers rather than grants: "It is not valid to object that some might prefer to use the money in the future rather than in the present. That is not the issue here, which is one of availability for use" (MES p. 386 n. 11). The objector is the man for whom this condition fails — he would hold the present sum until the date the promise pays.

Three findings are in this step. First, the number of ounces does no work in it. Equal counts are the same good at two dates only if they buy the same thing, and that is what the proof uses, so none of the conditions above says the sums are the same amount. The count enters later, where "repaid no more than lent" has to be compared with a promise, and it is a condition of its own there. (The two conditions once each held an equality of counts that this step never used. The linter checks whole assumptions and not their parts, so it could not see that; the counts were moved out by hand.) Second, two things a reader might fold together are kept apart: that the money will buy as much later, which fails with rising prices, and that the promise will pay, which fails with a risk of default. Each has its own name, so a failure points at one of them. Third, what the step needs about holding is that the present sum would be spent sooner. That holding the present sum costs nothing is in no signature: no step uses it, so a theorem that listed it would fail the linter. (A cost of holding cash does reach the loan theorems, by another road: the condition that refusing a loan leaves the sum at full value, below.) Rothbard's footnote dismisses the man who would keep the money for later, as "not the issue here, which is one of availability for use" (MES, p. 386 n. 11). If that man would spend the present sum on the same thing, at the same date, as the promise, the two buy the same bundle, and asymmetry then forbids a strict preference either way. The footnote answers him with an equality, not a preference.

"More money is preferred", the claim the next step needs, is spent on one thing only: ruling out a negative rate.

One ounce or less. "Or less" needs a second claim. Rothbard: "[T]o both present money and future money there applies the general rule that more of a good will have greater utility than less of it" (MES, p. 379). It is the tenth claim under The claims, where its docstring gives the reasons it is narrower than Rothbard's rule. Two of them are findings of the build.

Mises denies non-satiation of the cash a man holds: "He who says that his thirst for more money can never be quenched, does not mean to say that his cash holding can never be too large. Four claims carry it: time preference, "more money is preferred", asymmetry, and demonstrated preference, stated of the lender's trade. The borrower contributes nothing. That every rate struck is positive is a fact about each lender, not about a market, and a borrower who would pay a positive rate is not shown to exist.

What the order of time was asked for. Nothing, as under Time preference. No proof here uses a property of the relation "before", and none needs a classical step: the one case split is on the size of a sum.

The case. The library builds one frame to test this on. A lender with a use for an ounce now lends it for a promise of two, repaid at the next date, and every claim and condition of the last theorem holds.

The test is thin, and it is worth saying where. The lender's ranking does not change with the date, so the condition on ranking now as at the spending holds with nothing to do. The money promised is the promises themselves, so that the promise pays is never tried on a promise that might not. Every unit is money and every sum is free to hold, so neither condition is tried. Preference chains throughout the frame, not only on the one chain. The promise whose parts must each buy something different is a single unit, so it is tried only against nothing at all. And there is one agent, lender and borrower both.

What a failure would point at. Where a man lends and is repaid less, the theorem for a rate not above zero says that his trade did not demonstrate a preference for being repaid over keeping the sum, or that one of its assumptions failed. The ones a case is likely to land on: The money would buy less at repayment, or he doubted the promise would pay, or he had no use for the sum before the promise paid. Or refusing would not have left him the sum at full value, because keeping cash cost him something. (In the last theorem that condition is not assumed but derived, from the trade being voluntary and his holding counting as planned, so there the failure lands on one of those.) Or an extra unit of the promise bought nothing more, or he ranked the promise against the repayment differently now than at the spending, or the promise was not money or not free to hold, or the one chain of preferences did not chain. Or one of the claims failed. (Its manifest has a few more, of bookkeeping: that the promise and the repayment come from the stock of promises, that the promise is as many units as the sum lent, that its units are interchangeable, and that what he holds after counts as planned.)

The bank that lends overnight at a negative rate is likely to land on two of these. One is its use for the money: it may have had none before the loan was repaid, a day later, and where it had none the theorem says nothing more about it. The other is refusal: refusing would not have left it the sum at full value. A bank that refused kept the sum as reserves at the central bank, which charged for them, or as banknotes, which have to be guarded. Either failure takes the bank outside the theorem; neither is shown here to be the only way out.

Rothbard's own terms give an Austrian a reply for one part of this, though he never made it about negative rates. A deposit of present money is like goods left in a warehouse, and redeeming it "is not the completion of a transaction or equivalent to a creditor’s calling his loan; it is a simple repossessing of a man’s own good" (MES, p. 800). The man who deposits goods "pays the owner of the warehouse a certain sum for the service of storage. He still retains ownership of the property; the owner of the warehouse is simply guarding it" (MES, p. 801). On that reading, a negative rate on a deposit is a fee for safekeeping and not interest at all, and the theorems never applied to it. The reply does not reach the loan between two banks, which is a promise of money tomorrow, a credit transaction on Rothbard's own terms. But the fee does reach the lender's refusal: it is what keeping the sum would have cost. For banknotes the cost is plainly one of keeping the sum safe, a price for guarding and not for time. For reserves it turns on whether the central bank's own overnight deposit is a warehouse or a loan: a warehouse makes its charge a fee for safekeeping, and a loan makes it a negative rate in its own right. That is left open here, and nothing rests on it: from June 2014 the same charge also fell on the reserves a bank held above the required minimum in its ordinary account at the central bank. Those reserves do not stand for money stored elsewhere; they are the money itself, as banknotes are. So a bank that refused the overnight loan had nowhere to keep the sum for nothing. All of this is a reading of the case, not a frame built for it.

Where a man lends at exactly zero, the list is shorter: it drops the claim that more money is preferred and the five conditions that come with it.

The Christmas club saver lends at zero, and on this reading of a sum he is outside the theorem at once. The cash in his hand would buy what he would do with it on impulse, the club pays for what he saves it for, and those are not the same satisfaction. So whatever money the club's promise pays in, if it pays, the condition that his cash and that money have the same purchasing power fails. The library builds him too, with every claim holding:
