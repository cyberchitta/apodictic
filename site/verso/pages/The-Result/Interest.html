1.14. Interest🔗

Interest is the fifth target, and it is the one time preference was built for. Rothbard's conclusion is categorical: "every man’s time preference is positive, i.e., one ounce of present money will always be preferred to one ounce or less of future money. Therefore, there will never be any question of a zero or negative pure interest rate" (MES, p. 382). It was chosen because the world seems to have answered it. For some years bonds paid a negative nominal yield, and people bought them and held them to maturity: they lent a sum to be repaid less. The question is which step of the argument such a lender falls outside.

Rothbard's argument runs in four steps, and they are rebuilt here in order. Present money is preferred to the same amount of future money. So it is preferred to that amount or less. So a man does not lend at a rate of zero or below. So every rate actually struck is positive. The docstrings below label them S2 to S5. The rate as a number set in a market is not reached: nothing here adds up lenders or finds an equilibrium.

Money. The vocabulary gets two more relations, which units are money and whether holding a sum costs anything, and nothing else.

Money m: the unit m is an amount of money, or a claim to an amount of money at a later date. Money is Mises's: "the thing which serves as the generally accepted and commonly used medium of exchange" (Human Action, ch. XVII, §3). Supplied; no properties: whether a unit is money is a fact about the situation, checked by whoever applies a theorem, and each theorem that needs it names it as a hypothesis.

FreeToHold : self.Agent → self.Time → Finset self.Means → Prop

FreeToHold agent t sum: at t, holding exactly the units in sum costs the agent nothing — no storage, custody, tax or risk of loss eats into it. Supplied; no properties.

A sum of money counts for what the man's plan says he would buy with exactly those units. That is not how goods in a trade are counted under Exchange, where a holding counts for every end any of its goods could serve. For money the two part at once: an ounce can be spent now or kept for later, not both. Three stocks are weighed at the one date of the loan: the present money, the claims to future money, and the money the claims promise, as he would spend it were it in hand. A theorem takes a sum from each.

Present money over future money. "It follows from the law of time preference that present money is worth more than present expectations of the same amount of future money" (MES, p. 376). It follows, but not from the amount.

Present money is preferred to the same amount of future money (S2; MES p. 376: "It follows from the law of time preference that present money is worth more than present expectations of the same amount of future money").

"The same amount" does no work here: what the sums would buy does (SamePurchasingPower), and that the claim pays (ClaimPays). And the conclusion needs the present sum to be spent sooner — the case Rothbard's n. 11 sets aside.

Same purchasing power — a condition on the situation. Two sums are the same amount, and each would buy one satisfaction, the same satisfaction, each at its own date. Asserted in the theorems between the present sum and the money a claim promises.

Mises: "present goods are valued higher than future goods of the same kind and quantity" (Human Action, ch. XIX, §1). Rothbard's "the same amount of future money" (MES p. 376) does no work by itself: equal counts of ounces are the same good at two dates only if they buy the same thing. It fails where the money buys less at the later date (Rothbard's purchasing-power component, MES p. 797; Mises's price premium). Whether the claim pays is the separate condition ClaimPays.

Spent sooner — a condition on the situation. The present sum would be spent before the claim pays, and not on something already past at now.

It is what Rothbard's footnote answers rather than grants: "It is not valid to object that some might prefer to use the money in the future rather than in the present. That is not the issue here, which is one of availability for use" (MES p. 386 n. 11). The objector is the man for whom this condition fails — he would hold the present sum until the date the claim pays.

Three findings are in this step. First, the number of ounces does no work in it. The first two conditions above each contain an equality of counts, and the proof never uses either; the linter, which checks whole assumptions and not their parts, cannot see that. Equal counts are the same good at two dates only if they buy the same thing, and that is what the proof uses; the count enters later, where "repaid no more than lent" has to be compared with a claim. Second, two things a reader might fold together are kept apart: that the money will buy as much later, which fails with rising prices, and that the claim will pay, which fails with a risk of default. Each has its own name, so a failure points at one of them. A frame of one agent with an ounce and a claim to one shows each failing while the other holds, and shows that a single condition asserted between the cash and the claim fails alike in both cases, so it cannot say which happened:

Third, what the step needs about holding is that the present sum would be spent sooner. That holding the present sum costs nothing is in no signature: no step uses it, so a theorem that listed it would fail the linter. (A cost of holding cash does reach the loan theorems, by another road: the condition that refusing a loan leaves the sum at full value, below.) Rothbard's footnote dismisses the man who would keep the money for later, as "not the issue here, which is one of availability for use" (MES, p. 386 n. 11). If that man would spend the present sum on the same thing, at the same date, as the claim, the two buy the same bundle, and asymmetry then forbids a strict preference either way. The footnote answers him with an equality, not a preference.

"More money is preferred", the claim the next step needs, is spent on one thing only: ruling out a negative rate.

One ounce or less. "Or less" needs a second claim. Rothbard: "[T]o both present money and future money there applies the general rule that more of a good will have greater utility than less of it" (MES, p. 379). It is the tenth claim under The claims, where its docstring gives the reasons it is narrower than Rothbard's rule. Two of them are findings of the build.

The first is satiation. Read as Rothbard states it, even cut to money free to hold and to sums one part of the other, the rule ranks the larger sum above the smaller whatever each would buy:

Mises denies non-satiation of the cash a man holds: "He who says that his thirst for more money can never be quenched, does not mean to say that his cash holding can never be too large. Four claims carry it: time preference, "more money is preferred", asymmetry, and demonstrated preference, stated of the lender's trade. The borrower contributes nothing. That every rate struck is positive is a fact about each lender, not about a market, and a borrower who would pay a positive rate is not shown to exist.

What the order of time was asked for. Nothing, as under Time preference. No proof here uses a property of the relation "before", and none needs a classical step: the one case split is on the size of a sum.

The case. The library builds one frame to test this on. A lender with a use for an ounce now lends it for a claim to two, repaid at the next date, and every claim and condition of the last theorem holds.

The test is thin, and it is worth saying where. The lender's ranking does not change with the date, so the condition on ranking now as at the spending holds with nothing to do. The money the claims promise is the claims themselves, so that the claim pays is never tried on a claim that might not. Every unit is money and every sum is free to hold, so neither condition is tried. Preference chains throughout the frame, not only on the one chain. The claim whose parts must each buy something different is a single unit, so it is tried only against nothing at all. And there is one agent, lender and borrower both.

What a failure would point at. Where a man lends and is repaid less, the theorem for a rate not above zero says that his trade did not demonstrate a preference for the claim, or that one of its assumptions failed. The ones a case is likely to land on: The money would buy less at repayment, or he doubted the claim would pay, or he had no use for the sum before the claim paid. Or refusing would not have left him the sum at full value, because keeping cash cost him something. (In the last theorem that condition is not assumed but derived, from the trade being voluntary and his holding counting as planned, so there the failure lands on one of those.) Or an extra unit of the claim bought nothing more, or he ranked the claim against the repayment differently now than at the spending, or the claim was not money or not free to hold, or the one chain of preferences did not chain. Or one of the claims failed. (Its manifest has a few more, of bookkeeping: that the claim and the repayment come from the stock of claims, that its units are interchangeable, and that what he holds after counts as planned.)

The bondholder at a negative yield points first at his use for the money: he had none sooner than the bond paid. Held to that date instead, the cash would have bought at least what the smaller repayment buys. So lending at a loss makes sense only if holding the cash would have cost him something, and that points next at refusal: refusing would not have left him the sum at full value. This is a reading of the case, not a frame built for it. Where a man lends at exactly zero, the list is shorter: it drops the claim that more money is preferred and the five conditions that come with it.

The Christmas club saver lends at zero, and on this reading of a sum he is outside the theorem at once. The cash in his hand would buy what he would do with it on impulse, the club pays for what he saves it for, and those are not the same satisfaction. So whatever money the club's claim pays in, if it pays, the condition that his cash and that money have the same purchasing power fails. The library builds him too, with every claim holding:
