1.1. The horses🔗

Rothbard derives the law of marginal utility over pp. 24–27 of Man, Economy, and State. He works one case through: a man who owns six horses. This document follows that case throughout. The Lean comes back to the same six horses once the theorems are stated. The point is that you can hold his paragraph beside the formal statement. Then you can judge for yourself whether they say the same thing.

He sets the case up twice. The first time is by acquisition (MES p. 24):

The first horse will fulfill the most urgent wants that a horse can serve; this follows from the universal fact that action uses scarce means to satisfy the most urgent of the not yet satisfied wants.

The second time is for his diagram. This time he counts the ends (pp. 25–26):

We assume for simplicity that there are 10 ends which the means could fulfill, and that each unit of means is capable of serving one of the ends. If the supply of the good is 6 units, then the first six ends, ranked in order of importance by the valuing individual, are the ones that are being satisfied. Ends ranked 7–10 remain unsatisfied.

And he takes a horse away (p. 25):

Assume that a man has a supply of six (interchangeable) horses. … Suppose that he is now faced with the necessity of giving up one horse. … Obviously, he gives up the least urgent of the wants which the larger stock would have satisfied. Thus, if the individual was using one horse for pleasure riding, and he considers this the least important of his wants that were fulfilled by the six horses, the loss of a horse will cause him to give up pleasure riding.

That is the whole derivation. Everything below tries to say it in a form a machine will check.

Three pieces of vocabulary carry the argument. They are worth naming now, because the argument's shape turns on the third.

  • The stock (Stock): the six horses, and the ends a horse can serve at all.

  • The plan (AllocationPlan): for any set of horses he might have, which wants he would serve with them.

  • The preference the plan expresses (Prefers). It ranks bundles of wants, and nothing more is assumed of it. It is not assumed transitive. It is not assumed complete. No numbers are attached.

The full definitions are under The vocabulary. Nothing before that part depends on reading them.

The plan is where the trouble starts. It is worth seeing why now, before the formal statement makes it look inevitable.

Rothbard's argument compares six horses with five. But the man has six. What he would do with five is not anything he does. It is a plan, and it covers stocks he does not own. His own words give it away: "which the larger stock would have satisfied".

His first passage is about acquiring horses one after another. It can be read with no plan at all, as a history of what he did with each. That reading returns under The ladder as a history.

A second horse passage decides how the plan has to be written down (p. 27):

suppose that the sixth horse that he had previously acquired (named "Seabiscuit") he had placed in the service of pleasure riding. Suppose that he now must lose another horse ("Man o' War") which had arrived earlier, and which was engaged in the more important duty (to him) of leading a wagon. He will still give up end 6 by simply transferring Seabiscuit from this function to the wagon-leading end.

Rothbard names two horses to insist that which horse goes makes no difference. That is only worth saying if the plan could have depended on which. So here the plan is indexed by which horses. A separate named condition says only the number matters (plan.Homogeneous).

Both of his conclusions are proved of the six horses, under The horses in Lean:

  • The loss of a horse ends pleasure riding (Model.loss_of_a_horse_ends_pleasure_riding).

  • Which horse goes makes no difference (Model.which_horse_does_not_matter).
