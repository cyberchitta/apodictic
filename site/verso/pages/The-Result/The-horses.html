1.1. The horses🔗

Terms such as library, claim and condition are explained under The Result.

Rothbard derives the law of marginal utility over pp. 24–27 of Man, Economy, and State. He states it on p. 27:

The greater the supply of a good, the lower the marginal utility; the smaller the supply, the higher the marginal utility.

He says what the marginal utility is: "This least important end fulfilled by the stock is known as … marginal utility" (p. 27). Utility, he adds, is "expressible only in terms of higher or lower order in the preferences of men" (p. 28).

He works one case through: a man who owns six horses. This document follows that case throughout. The library comes back to the same six horses once the theorems are stated. The point is that you can hold his paragraph beside the formal statement. Then you can judge for yourself whether they say the same thing.

He sets the case up twice and takes a horse away. The first set-up adds horses one at a time (MES p. 24). This builds the law upward, as the stock grows:

The first horse will fulfill the most urgent wants that a horse can serve; this follows from the universal fact that action uses scarce means to satisfy the most urgent of the not yet satisfied wants.

He then writes that "for all human actions, as the quantity of the supply (stock) of a good increases, the utility (value) of each additional unit decreases" (p. 25).

Between the two set-ups he takes a horse away (p. 25). This takes the law downward, from six horses to five:

Assume that a man has a supply of six (interchangeable) horses. … Suppose that he is now faced with the necessity of giving up one horse. … Obviously, he gives up the least urgent of the wants which the larger stock would have satisfied. Thus, if the individual was using one horse for pleasure riding, and he considers this the least important of his wants that were fulfilled by the six horses, the loss of a horse will cause him to give up pleasure riding.

The second set-up numbers the ends, for a diagram of the value scale (his Figure 3, not shown here). The numbers are used later: "end 6" is the sixth-ranked end (pp. 25–26):

We assume for simplicity that there are 10 ends which the means could fulfill, and that each unit of means is capable of serving one of the ends. If the supply of the good is 6 units, then the first six ends, ranked in order of importance by the valuing individual, are the ones that are being satisfied. Ends ranked 7–10 remain unsatisfied.

These passages carry the derivation. One more comes below. Everything below tries to say them in a form a machine will check.

Three pieces of vocabulary carry the argument. They are worth naming now, because the argument's shape turns on the second.

  • The stock (Stock): the six horses, and the ends a horse can serve at all.

  • The plan (AllocationPlan): for any set of horses he might have, which wants he would serve with them.

  • The preference (Prefers): his ranking of one bundle of wants above another. Here a want and an end are the same thing (End). A bundle is a set of them. Nothing more is assumed of the ranking. It is not assumed transitive: A over B and B over C need not put A over C. It is not assumed complete: two bundles need not be ranked either way. No numbers are attached.

Rothbard's argument is one of two routes to the law (see The Result). On it the law needs one claim: swap dominance (SwapDominant). It ties the plan to the ranking. Take any set of his horses. Swap any want the plan would serve for any want a horse could serve but the plan would leave unserved. Then he prefers what the plan would serve to what the swap leaves him.

Ranking bundles, not single wants, is what lets the step from bundles to single wants be a named condition (IndependentUses).

A property of the ranking is added only when some theorem forces it. On this route the law forces neither transitivity nor completeness. The order among single wants comes from swap dominance (SwapDominant), with independence of uses (IndependentUses). See The theorems and The other findings.

The full definitions are under The vocabulary. The parts between here and that one can be read without them.

The plan is where the trouble starts. It is worth seeing why now, before the formal statement makes it look inevitable.

Rothbard's argument compares six horses with five. But the man has six. What he would do with five is not anything he does. It is a plan, and it covers stocks he does not own. His own words give it away: "which the larger stock would have satisfied".

The trouble is not that the plan covers such stocks. Rothbard reasons about them himself. The trouble is that the plan is settled in advance. It answers for every set of horses at once. It is not a choice he makes, and no act of his settles it. That matters because Rothbard holds that "value scales do not exist in a void apart from the concrete choices of action" (p. 33). On the same page he exempts this law from that rule, with no argument. The case is made under The finding.

His first passage is about acquiring horses one after another. It can be read with no plan at all, as a history of what he did with each. That reading returns under The ladder as a history.

A later passage (p. 27) decides how the plan has to be written down:

suppose that the sixth horse that he had previously acquired (named "Seabiscuit") he had placed in the service of pleasure riding. Suppose that he now must lose another horse ("Man o' War") which had arrived earlier, and which was engaged in the more important duty (to him) of leading a wagon. He will still give up end 6 by simply transferring Seabiscuit from this function to the wagon-leading end.

End 6 here is pleasure riding, the sixth of his ranked ends. Seabiscuit is the sixth horse in order of arrival.

Rothbard names two horses to insist that which horse goes makes no difference. That is only worth saying if the plan could have depended on which. So in the library the plan says what he would serve with each particular set of horses. It is not indexed by a number of horses (AllocationPlan). That only the number matters is a separate condition (plan.Homogeneous). It says any two sets of his horses of the same size would serve the same wants. It is assumed only where a theorem needs it.

The library builds a model of the six horses. Both of his conclusions are proved as theorems about it, under The horses in Lean:

  • The loss of a horse ends pleasure riding (Model.loss_of_a_horse_ends_pleasure_riding).

  • Which horse goes makes no difference (Model.which_horse_does_not_matter).

Neither takes a condition. The second rests on a theorem that the model's plan is interchangeable (horses_homogeneous). There the condition is proved, not assumed.
