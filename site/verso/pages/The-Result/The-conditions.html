1.5. The conditions🔗

Terms such as library, claim, condition and frame are explained under The Result.

The law of marginal utility is read three ways here. Two are the routes: Rothbard's and Mises's. The third is the ladder read as a history. All three are introduced under The Result. The third has a part of its own, The ladder as a history. Across the three, the law takes five conditions besides the claims (see The claims). Each says something about the situation, not about action as such. Each is written into the statement of every theorem that needs it. So a reader can point at one and say: that is the one that did not hold. Where one fails, the law says nothing. It is silent, not wrong.

The list speaks of the man's plan (AllocationPlan). For each set of his units, the plan says which wants he would serve with exactly those units. Such a set is a sub-stock. The plan is under The vocabulary.

  • One more unit (stock.OneMore fewer more). The two piles compared are both sub-stocks: both are cut from the units on hand. They differ by exactly one unit. So the theorems that carry it compare only parts of the man's own stock. Rothbard's route and Mises's route carry it. The ladder read as a history does not.

  • Independence of uses (IndependentUses agent time). A bundle is a set of wants. Take two bundles that differ in one want only: the first has want X where the second has want Y. If he prefers the first bundle to the second, he prefers X to Y. Put loosely: what one want is worth does not depend on which other wants are being served. Of the three readings, only Rothbard's route carries it.

  • Interchangeability of units (plan.Homogeneous). The plan depends only on how many units there are, not on which ones. On Rothbard's route the law has two forms (see The theorems). The chain form (marginal_utility_chain) names which units. The size-based form (marginal_utility) compares supplies by size. Only the size-based form carries this condition. No theorem on Mises's route carries it.

  • Comparability (stock.ComparableServiceable). Any two wants the good can serve are ranked one way or the other. Mises's route needs it (Mises.ladder_from_order). So does the ladder read as a history (Temporal.ladder_judged_earlier). No theorem on Rothbard's route carries it. That route's claim is swap dominance (SwapDominant, under The claims). At any sub-stock, swap one want the plan serves for one the good can serve but the plan leaves unserved. He prefers the wants the plan would serve there to the wants left after the swap. With independence of uses, this ranks some pairs of wants. At any sub-stock, he prefers every want the plan would serve to every want the good can serve that the plan would leave unserved (served_over_unserved, under The theorems). Every pair of wants that route's theorems compare is of that kind: one served at a sub-stock, one left unserved there. That is so for urgency_principle directly, and for the chain form through it. For the size-based form it holds once interchangeability of units (plan.Homogeneous) makes the plan the same at its two sub-stocks of one size. So those pairs are ranked without comparability. What stays unranked is under Mises's route.

  • No new uses (earlier.NoNewUses later). At the later moment, the good is believed to serve no use it was not believed to serve at the earlier one. Only the ladder read as a history needs it. There it is the one condition that spans two moments. It is about what he believes the good can do, not about how he ranks his wants. And it runs one way: whatever is believed later was believed earlier, not the reverse. The man learns no new use, though he may drop an old one.

No theorem here has to pick out which plan is the man's. Each is handed a plan, and its statement is about that plan. The other way would be a claim about every plan the man might have. Such a claim can be refuted by construction. From any one plan a neighbour can be built: the same plan, changed by one swap at one set of units. Take the claim that every such plan satisfies swap dominance. If preference is asymmetric, the neighbour of a plan that satisfies it does not (no_rival_swap_dominant, under The theorems). So nothing here is said of every plan. The one condition here that speaks of a plan (plan.Homogeneous) ranges over every sub-stock. But it is a condition on the one plan the theorem is handed. So there is no rival plan anyone could build to refute the theorem. This is the rule of method under The other findings.

Each of the five is shown below: first its Lean definition, then its docstring. They come in the order of the list above. They are shown whole because these are the conditions a reader has to judge.

Other parts take conditions of their own:

  • Exchange takes conditions about trades rather than stocks. They are given in full under Exchange.

  • Time preference takes its own too, about dated ends. They are given in full under Time preference.

  • The regression theorem's are about a record of exchanges. They are given in full under The regression theorem.

  • The interest theorems' are about sums of money and a loan. They are given in full under Interest. Some of the interest theorems take interchangeability of units too. They take it of his plan for a stock of future money (futurePlan.Homogeneous).

more is fewer plus one unit, both inside the stock. A sub-stock is a set of the stock's units. The definition says three things: fewer is inside more, more is inside the stock, and more has one unit more than fewer. It does not name the extra unit. So every sub-stock here is one a theorem is handed. None is built from another by adding a named unit. In Lean, adding a unit to a finite set needs a test of whether that unit is already in it: a test of when two units are the same unit (decidable equality of units). An arbitrary frame does not supply that test. A theorem could take it as an extra argument, as the library's theorems take decidable equality of ends ([DecidableEq praxis.End]). Or it could take it from Lean's classical axiom (Classical.choice), which no theorem outside Consistency.lean uses. Stated with an inclusion and a count, as here, nothing needs the test, so neither is needed.

🔗def
Apodictic.ActionFrame.IndependentUses (praxis : ActionFrame)
  (agent : praxis.Agent) (time : praxis.Time) : Prop
Apodictic.ActionFrame.IndependentUses
  (praxis : ActionFrame)
  (agent : praxis.Agent)
  (time : praxis.Time) : Prop

Independence of uses — a condition on the situation, NOT a universal claim. A bundle is a set of ends. Take a set of ends, and two ends outside it, want and other. Suppose he prefers the set with want added to the set with other added. Then he prefers want to other. So a preference between two bundles that differ in exactly one end carries down to a preference between those two ends. It fails where uses are complementary. Say end B is worth having only alongside end A, and A is in the set. He may prefer the set with B added to the set with some C added. Yet on their own he may not prefer B to C. Then this condition fails. It is named here so that the theorems that need it carry it as a hypothesis. It is also named so that a reader can point at it where it does not hold.

This is NOT a premise Rothbard's argument uses. He ranks wants against each other directly. He reads their ranking off his value scale (MES pp. 25–26, Figure 3). He takes that ranking as given. He never argues from bundles at all. He makes one fused claim, of single wants: "the universal fact that action uses scarce means to satisfy the most urgent of the not yet satisfied wants" (MES p. 24). What it says of the order of wants we split in two: a claim about bundles (swap dominance, SwapDominant) and this condition. Its other half, that action employs the means, is left out (see SwapDominant). The split is OURS, and this condition is its cost. Complementarity never comes up in his wording. Once the claim is split, it comes up here, as the case where this condition fails. To assert this condition rather than hypothesize it would be to assert that complementarity never happens.

Interchangeability of units — a condition on the situation, NOT a praxeological claim. The plan depends only on how many units there are, not on which ones. Any two sub-stocks of the same size would serve the same ends.

A sub-stock is a set of the stock's units. Marginal utility is read off the plan: the ends it would serve with the larger of two sub-stocks and not with the smaller (marginalEnds). Without this condition, the phrase "the plan at n units" picks out nothing in particular. So neither does the marginal utility of a supply of n.

Rothbard makes interchangeability part of what a supply IS: "If a specific unit is differently evaluated from all other units, then the supply of that good is only one unit" (MES p. 23). On his account, where it fails, the units are not one good. The library does not build it in. The size-based form of the law (Apodictic.marginal_utility) carries it as a hypothesis. So where it fails, that form is silent about these units. It does not treat them as one.

Apodictic.Stock.ComparableServiceable

  {time : praxis.Time}
  (stock : Stock praxis agent time) : Prop

Comparability of the ends a good can serve — a condition on the situation, NOT a praxeological claim.

Any two ends the good can serve are ranked one way or the other. It is confined to one stock's serviceable ends, because that is all any theorem needs. It is not asserted of every pair of ends in the frame.

Where it fails, a theorem carrying it is SILENT rather than false. An agent may simply never have had occasion to rank two ends against each other. Rothbard's own doctrine is that scales are revealed in action and nowhere else. That doctrine makes such agents the normal case rather than the exotic one. Mises says the same thing in almost the same words: "the scale of values or wants manifests itself only in the reality of action. These scales have no independent existence apart from the actual behavior of individuals" (Human Action, ch. IV, §2).

It is carried by the Mises route (Apodictic.Mises.ladder_from_order). It is carried by the ladder read as a history (Apodictic.Temporal.ladder_judged_earlier). It is carried by no theorem on the Rothbard route. That asymmetry is a finding: Mises's route needs a condition on the agent's value scale that Rothbard's route does not.

Swap dominance (SwapDominant), with independence of uses (IndependentUses), already ranks some pairs. Take any sub-stock on hand. Every end the plan would serve with it is preferred to every end the good can serve that the plan would leave unserved with it (Apodictic.served_over_unserved). So where both hold, this condition can fail only for a pair that no sub-stock splits: at every sub-stock, the plan serves both or neither. A frame where swap dominance holds and this condition fails is built in Apodictic.Model.rothbard_premise_holds_where_mises_is_silent.

Apodictic.Stock.NoNewUses {praxis : ActionFrame} {agent : praxis.Agent}

  (earlier : Stock praxis agent earlierTime)
  (later : Stock praxis agent laterTime) : Prop
Apodictic.Stock.NoNewUses
  {praxis : ActionFrame}
  {agent : praxis.Agent}
  {earlierTime laterTime : praxis.Time}
  (earlier :
    Stock praxis agent earlierTime)
  (later : Stock praxis agent laterTime) :
  Prop

The good is believed to serve no new use — whatever the good is believed to serve at the later moment, it was believed to serve at the earlier one. The later stock's serviceable ends are among the earlier stock's.

It is a situational condition. It is the one condition on the history reading that spans two moments. It runs one way. It is about BELIEF about the good, not about the scale of values. Between the two moments the man learns no new use for the good.

Its theorem is the ladder judged on the earlier scale (Apodictic.Temporal.ladder_judged_earlier). That theorem ranks a want on the earlier scale, the ranking he held at the earlier moment. The want is one served at the later moment and not at the earlier one. Where the supply grew by one unit between the two moments (Apodictic.Stock.Grows), it is the want the added unit goes to. The theorem does not need the growth as a hypothesis. That want has to be among the wants the good was believed able to serve at the earlier moment. Only of those wants do the theorem's other two hypotheses speak: comparability (earlier.ComparableServiceable) and acting in order (ActsInOrder before). This condition puts it there. Where he does learn a new use, that theorem is silent.

Does not say that the good's uses stay the same. A use believed in earlier may be dropped later. It says nothing about the scale at either moment. It says nothing about which units are held. Nor does it say which moment came first. Read as a history, the earlier moment comes first, and "learns" is said in that reading. In the Lean, "earlier" and "later" only name its two arguments. The one way it runs is the inclusion: later uses among earlier ones. The frame (ActionFrame) puts no order on its times (see Apodictic.Stock.Grows). It is carried by Apodictic.Temporal.ladder_judged_earlier and by nothing else.
