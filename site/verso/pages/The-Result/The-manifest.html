1.7. The manifest🔗

Terms such as library, signature, claim, condition, linter and vocabulary are explained under The Result.

This part prints the manifest of the law of marginal utility, in its size-based form (marginal_utility, under The theorems). Everything that theorem carries is here, in one place. Nothing outside this list is assumed. The list is not compiled by reading the source. Nor does it rest on a copy of a command's output. The command (#manifest) itself runs each time this page is built. The page's source also holds a copy of what it prints. The build checks that the two match. So what follows is what the command printed.

Read it by kind. The sort is what the audit turns on. If the claim is false, praxeology has asserted something false, and the law loses its proof on this route. Where a condition does not hold, the law says nothing there. It is silent, not wrong. Telling which of the two gave way in a particular case is what the audit is for (see The finding).

The printed manifest has five headed lists, then the logical background. Each line in the first four lists is a binder: one thing the statement takes as an argument. The four are the praxeological claims, the situational conditions, the conditions on the data, and the vocabulary (what the claims are about). The fifth lists fields, not binders. The bullets below take the lists in turn.

  • The one praxeological claim is swap dominance (SwapDominant, under The claims). It is a premise meant to hold of all action, not only in some situations. On this route the law is derived from it.

  • The situational conditions are what an Austrian points at to say the law did not apply here. The size form compares two one-unit steps (see The theorems). The first runs from a set of units belowSmaller to a set smaller, of n units. The second runs from belowLarger to larger, of n + 1. Three of the conditions listed are among the five set out under The conditions: independence of uses (IndependentUses agent time), interchangeability of units (plan.Homogeneous) and one more unit (stock.OneMore). One more unit appears twice, once for each step (stepToSmaller, stepToLarger). The other two lines (smallerSize, largerSize) are not among the five. They are bookkeeping. They fix which two supplies are being compared: one of n units, one of n + 1. They are printed under this heading because the command puts there every hypothesis that is neither a claim nor a condition on the data.

  • The condition on the data ([DecidableEq praxis.End]) says only that two ends can be told apart: for any two ends, whether they are the same end can be decided.

  • The conditions carried by the vocabulary are not binders at all. They are fields of the structures the theorem takes as arguments: the stock (stock.unitsAlike) and the plan (plan.servesOnlyWhatItCan). So whoever supplies a stock and a plan has already discharged them. They are preconditions of using the law in every sense that matters. But they do not show in the signature. So they are where an encoding could hide an assumption. #manifest prints them, so these two are not hidden. They are set out again under The vocabulary.

The line headed logical background is Lean's own: the axioms the theorem depends on. propext and Quot.sound arrive with ordinary mathematics. A proof that uses mathlib's finite sets brings both. So does one that uses extensionality for sets. Neither axiom says anything about economics (see The Result). Classical.choice is different, and it is absent. It is the axiom behind classical logic. With it, every question counts as having a yes-or-no answer, whether or not anything settles it. No proof here fills a gap that way.

The proofs do split into cases. But they split only on whether two ends are the same end. A case split needs a yes-or-no answer. Without Classical.choice, the logic does not supply one in general. So this one answer is asked for in the statement, as the condition on the data ([DecidableEq praxis.End]). Had a proof used Classical.choice instead, the statement would not show it. The axiom would appear only on the logical background line, with nothing to say what it decided.

The size form needs interchangeability of units (plan.Homogeneous) for one thing. It says that smaller and belowLarger, two sets of n units, would serve the same ends. The law has a second form, the chain form (marginal_utility_chain, under The theorems). It names which units are compared: small inside medium inside large. Its two steps share a set, medium. So it needs no interchangeability of units, and no sizes. Its manifest has the same claim and the same condition on the data. Its situational conditions are independence of uses and two one-more-unit steps (_firstStep, secondStep). The _ mark is explained below. The urgency principle (urgency_principle, under The theorems) says this. Take a unit away. Every end he would still serve is preferred to every end he would give up. Its one step runs from a set fewer to a set more (stock.OneMore fewer more): more holds fewer and one unit besides, and lies inside the stock. It carries the same claim and the same kinds of condition as the chain form, with that one step in place of two. The chain form is worth printing too, because of one line in it:

#manifest derives both manifests from the theorem itself. It walks the signature and sorts every binder by kind. Then it reads one level into the vocabulary. There it reports the fields of the library's structures that are statements (in Lean, fields of type Prop), as against data such as a set of units. It decides that a binder is a praxeological claim by the name its type is built on: SwapDominant in SwapDominant plan. If that name is declared in the file Praxeology.lean, the binder is a claim. That is the project's own rule: every claim is a structure in Praxeology.lean. The command asks it of the compiler rather than of a maintainer. So a claim declared in the wrong place shows up as a situational condition. That is the mistake worth catching.

What #manifest will not do is sort what it finds one level down. Whether a carried condition could fail in a real situation is a judgement about the world. The command reads only the condition's statement in Lean. So it cannot make that judgement. The two carried conditions (stock.unitsAlike, plan.servesOnlyWhatItCan) are sorted by hand instead, under The vocabulary. That sorting is not checked by the machine. Nor is the list of part-used hypotheses below, which comes from reading the proofs. The lists themselves are checked. The compiler prints them when this document is built. The build compares each printed list with the copy in this page's source. If the two differ, the build fails. So this page cannot show a list the theorem does not have.

The lists show what each theorem takes. The linter checks that each binder does work (see The claims). Three things limit how much that settles. Each is real.

  • The linter is never switched off here. But switching it off is possible. So keeping it on is our practice, not something the machine guarantees.

  • The linter can also be silenced one binder at a time, by prefixing a hypothesis with _. Doing that is how we record that the hypothesis does nothing. marginal_utility_chain carries one such binder, _firstStep (stock.OneMore small medium). That it does nothing is itself a finding. Each hypothesis that does no work is recorded as one: the statement asks for more than the proof needs. This one shows that the chain form needs nothing of how small stands to medium: not that the first step adds one unit, nor even that small lies inside medium (see The theorems).

  • The linter judges each binder whole. So it cannot see a hypothesis only part of which is used. Both marginal-utility theorems have such hypotheses. They are listed below.

#manifest does nothing about the first limit: it does not run the linter. It closes the second limit. In the chain form's manifest above, the line for _firstStep ends with the mark -- listed, does no work. So the binder is listed, and marked, exactly where the linter has gone quiet. That is the line the chain form was printed for.

#manifest does not close the third limit. A part-used hypothesis looks whole to both. Reading the two proofs finds three:

  • Each form's conclusion begins "for every end marginal at the first step, …". So the conclusion carries a hypothesis of its own: that the end is marginal there (marginalEnds). (The paragraph after this list says more on binders in a conclusion.) Marginal means served at the step's larger set, and not served at its smaller one. Only the first half is used. In the chain form the first step is from small to medium. The conclusion still speaks of the ends that step adds, though _firstStep, the hypothesis that it is a one-unit step, does no work. The proof names the unused half _notNeeded. That name is inside the proof, not in the statement. So neither the linter nor #manifest shows it.

  • The chain form's second step (secondStep) is handed to the urgency principle. That proof uses only that the smaller supply is on hand: it lies inside the larger, which lies inside the stock. That the larger has exactly one unit more is not used.

  • The size form's first step (stepToSmaller), from belowSmaller to smaller, is used only for this: smaller lies inside the stock. That belowSmaller lies inside smaller is not used. Nor is it used that the step adds one unit.

And #manifest brings a limit of its own. Lean draws no line between a signature and a conclusion. A conclusion of the form "for every end in this set, …" (∀ x ∈ s, …) binds things too. It binds the end, and a hypothesis that the end is in the set. Lean gives that hypothesis no name of its own: it is an anonymous binder. The command takes the first anonymous binder, other than a condition on the data, to begin the conclusion. It cuts there, and reports how many binders it dropped. But a conclusion binder the author named, before that cut, is still listed under vocabulary. In the two manifests above, atSmaller and addedFirst are such binders: each is the first end the conclusion speaks of. So is least, in the manifest under The ladder as a history. They are binders, so they are printed under the vocabulary heading, "what the claims are about". But no claim is about them. When the command drops any binders, the manifest ends with a line in parentheses saying how many. When it drops none, there is no such line. Both manifests above end with one. The count does not say where the conclusion begins: a named binder of the conclusion can sit before the cut, as here. So where that line appears, read the statement too.

So the guarantee is narrower than "nothing here is idle", that is, "every hypothesis does work in the proof". With the linter on, it is this: no hypothesis the signature lists is wholly idle, unless its name starts with _. A hypothesis can still be partly idle, as the three above are. The statement does not say so. Neither the linter nor #manifest finds such a case. Reading the proofs does.
