1.9. The circle🔗

Terms such as library, claim and condition are explained under The Result.

Two derivations of one law, the law of marginal utility, have now been rebuilt. Both give way at the same place. The pieces are under The finding and Mises's route. This part sets them side by side. It says plainly what the audit establishes about them, and what it does not. It is short, because most of the work is already done.

Two words recur below. A sub-stock is a set of units cut from the man's stock (see The conditions). The plan (AllocationPlan) says, for each sub-stock, which wants he would serve with exactly those units (see The vocabulary).

Both deny needing what both use. Here are the two authors' rules about value scales:

  • Rothbard: value scales "do not exist in a void apart from the concrete choices of action", and "there is no need for him to formulate hypothetical value scales" (p. 33).

  • Mises: the scales have "no independent existence apart from the actual behavior of individuals" (ch. IV, §2).

They state the same rule. A man's ranking shows up in what he does, and nowhere else.

Then each author derives the law from a premise that rule forbids. The premises come first. Why the rule forbids them comes after.

  • Rothbard's premise (SwapDominant) ranks the wants the man's plan would serve. It does so for every sub-stock.

  • Mises's premise (ServedInOrder) is written over exactly the same sub-stocks. His route asks less of the man's plan and more of his ranking. The more is a condition: every pair of wants the good can serve is ranked, one way or the other (stock.ComparableServiceable; see Mises's route).

So the two premises (SwapDominant, ServedInOrder) range over the same region: every subset of the stock in hand. Neither reaches past it. They do speak of supplies he does not hold: the sub-stocks smaller than his stock. The obvious complaint is that this is the trouble. The finding shows the complaint fails:

  • Reasoning about such supplies is Rothbard's own practice.

  • One-unit steps down, of the kind he takes, reach every sub-stock from the stock in hand.

  • On p. 33, the line between what he allows and what he refuses is drawn around the stock in hand.

So how far the premises reach is not the trouble, on either route.

The trouble is what both premises are. Each is a claim about the plan. The plan is a settled answer, held in advance, for every one of those sub-stocks at once. It answers before any of the choosing. On the rule above, a scale shows up choice by concrete choice, as each choice is made (see The finding). So no act of his settles the plan. It is a scale that stands apart from the concrete choices. Both rules deny such a scale. The case is made for Rothbard under The finding, and for Mises under Mises's route.

What each then helps himself to. Rothbard sees the difficulty. The law says something about the man's choices before they are made. So does the plan. On p. 33, the page that states his rule, he says no one can predict with certainty the course of a man's choices, "except that they will follow the law of marginal utility, which was deduced from the axiom of action". That subordinate clause exempts the law from the rule, by name. He attaches no argument. The circle runs like this:

  • The clause says in advance what his choices will obey. The plan, too, says in advance what he would choose. So the clause is what lets him hand the man a standing plan: one settled in advance, for every sub-stock at once.

  • The law is then proved from that plan.

  • So the warrant for the premise is the law it is used to prove.

Mises meets the difficulty differently. He sets down his definition of the marginal employment (Mises.marginalEmployment). It is the employment a man makes at n units "but would not make if, other things being equal, his supply were only n–1 units". So it is counterfactual, as his premise is: it turns on what he would do if his supply were smaller. He prefaces the definition with a remark: "We do not transcend the sphere of praxeological reasoning in establishing the following definition" (ch. VII, §1). He follows it with a reason: "It follows necessarily from our assumptions that people act (choose) and that in the first case acting man has n units of a homogeneous supply and in the second case n–1 units." The reason takes the n–1 case as an assumption. It does not say what act would show the choice he would make there. And it is given for the definition, not for the premise.

Rothbard reasons in a circle: his warrant for the premise is the law itself. Mises assumes the counterfactual case, and gives his premise no warrant.

The weaker premise does not help. Mises's premise (ServedInOrder) is strictly weaker than two claims and a condition taken together:

  • Rothbard's premise (SwapDominant).

  • Independence of uses (IndependentUses).

  • Asymmetry of preference (AsymmetricPreference).

The three entail it (Contrast.rothbard_entails_servedInOrder). It does not entail Rothbard's premise (Model.servedInOrder_not_entail_swapDominant). Both theorems are under Mises's route. A reader might hope that weakening it further reaches something about actual choices alone. Here is the reason to expect not. On both routes the law compares two supplies at one moment. The man holds one of them. So whatever premise delivers the law at one moment has to say something about the supply he does not hold. The only thing that can be said about that supply is what he would do with it. That is the counterfactual tense: what he would do, not what he did. Weakening the premise changes how much it says in that tense. It cannot change the tense.

That is a reason, not a result. Nothing here proves that no premise about actual choices can reach the law. No one has tried to prove it. What the audit has is two routes. Each rests on a standing counterfactual that its author disowns.

A third reading drops the one moment. It speaks only of what the man did. On it, he held the smaller supply at an earlier moment. It needs no plan. It pays instead with conditions. One is that he learned no new use for the good between the two moments (earlier.NoNewUses later). The part called The ladder as a history sets it out.

One shape, if you like. What follows is a reading, not a finding. These are the two defects the audit turned up in Rothbard:

  • The circle, here.

  • The inference about units: from his definition of a supply (p. 23) to a plan that cares only how many units the man has. It is set out under The other findings.

The two have a common form. Each time, what is offered as a warrant does not reach what it is offered for:

  • The exemption is offered as the warrant for the standing plan. What it names is only the law.

  • Rothbard's definition of a supply is offered as the warrant for a plan that does not care which units the man has, only how many (plan.Homogeneous). What it reaches is a fact about the units only: each is equally able to serve (stock.unitsAlike). A fact about the plan does not follow (Model.unitsAlike_not_entail_homogeneous; see The finding).

Is that common form one habit, or two coincidences? A proof assistant cannot say.

What is and is not established. Established:

  • Each derivation follows from its stated premise and conditions.

  • Each premise is a claim about what the man would do.

  • Each author restricts value scales to what the man does do.

  • Neither author gives his premise a warrant that stands apart from the law.

Not established:

  • That the law is false.

  • That praxeology cannot have it.

One more fact sits beside the second. The third reading above has a theorem (Temporal.ladder_judged_earlier). It proves the ladder read as a history: each want the later allocation serves, and the earlier did not, ranks below the least urgent want the earlier one served (see The ladder as a history). It rests on acting in order (ActsInOrder), a claim about what the man actually does, and on no plan. It also takes two conditions. So a ladder does follow from a claim about action without a plan, but only on the scale he ranked by before the stock grew. On the later scale the ladder does not follow from acting in order (Model.later_judged_ladder_fails). What it reaches, and what it costs, is under The ladder as a history.

The audit began with a question. Is the certainty Mises claimed for this law the certainty of a theorem? The answer is as narrow as the two lists under What is and is not established. Each derivation is as certain as its premise. The premises are the part nobody has derived.
