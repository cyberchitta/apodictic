1.9. The circle🔗

Two derivations of one law have now been rebuilt. Both give way at the same place. The pieces are under The finding and Mises's route. This part sets them side by side. It says plainly what the audit establishes about them, and what it does not. It is short, because most of the work is already done.

Both deny needing what both use. Here are two sentences, one from each author:

  • Rothbard's man has "no need for him to formulate hypothetical value scales" (p. 33).

  • Mises's scales have "no independent existence apart from the actual behavior of individuals" (ch. IV, §2).

They state the same rule. A man's ranking shows up in what he does, and nowhere else.

Then each author derives the law from a premise that rule forbids:

  • Rothbard's premise (SwapDominant) ranks the wants the man would serve. It does so for every stable he could cut from his own.

  • Mises's route asks less of the man's plan and more of the ranking (stock.ComparableServiceable). Its premise (ServedInOrder) is written over exactly the same stables.

So the two premises (SwapDominant, ServedInOrder) range over the same region: every subset of the stock in hand. Neither reaches an inch past it. Reach was never the trouble, on either route. The trouble is what both premises are. Each is a settled answer, held in advance, for every one of those stables at once. That is the scale in advance that both authors say a man does not need.

What each then helps himself to. Rothbard sees the difficulty. He exempts the law from the rule by name, in a subordinate clause. The exemption's only warrant is the derivation. Mises does not see the difficulty, or does not say so. He sets down his counterfactual definition (Mises.marginalEmployment). He announces that in doing so "We do not transcend the sphere of praxeological reasoning" (ch. VII, §1). One reasons in a circle. The other declares. Neither gives the premise a warrant that stands apart from the law it is used to prove.

The weaker premise does not help. On the allocation, Mises's premise (ServedInOrder) is strictly the weaker. A reader might hope that weakening it further reaches something about actual choices alone. Here is the reason to expect not. The law compares two supplies. The man holds one of them. So whatever premise delivers the law at one moment has to say something about the supply he does not hold. The only thing that can be said about that supply is what he would do with it. Weakening the premise changes how much it says in that tense. It cannot change the tense.

That is a reason, not a result. Nothing here proves that no premise about actual choices can reach the law. No one has tried to prove it. What the audit has is two routes. Each rests on a standing counterfactual that its author disowns.

A third reading speaks only of what the man did. On it, he held the smaller supply at an earlier moment. It is set out under The ladder as a history. It pays in a different coin.

One shape, if you like. What follows is a reading, not a finding. It is offered as a reading. These are the two defects the audit turned up in Rothbard:

  • The circle, here.

  • The inference about units, set out under The other findings.

The two have a common form. Each time, what is offered as a warrant does not reach what it is offered for:

  • The exemption is offered for the plan. It reaches only the law.

  • The definition of a supply is offered for the plan not caring which units the man has (plan.Homogeneous). It reaches only the units.

Mises's declaration fits the same form trivially, since it reaches nothing. Is that one habit, or two coincidences? A proof assistant cannot say.

What is and is not established. Established:

  • Each derivation follows from its stated premise and conditions.

  • Each premise is a claim about what the man would do.

  • Each author restricts value scales to what the man does do.

  • Neither author warrants his premise.

Not established:

  • That the law is false.

  • That praxeology cannot have it.

A ladder theorem from actual action does exist, judged on the earlier scale (Temporal.ladder_judged_earlier). It rests on acting in order (ActsInOrder). What it reaches, and what it costs, is under The ladder as a history.

The audit began with a question. Is the certainty Mises claimed for this law the certainty of a theorem? The answer is as narrow as the two lists under What is and is not established. The derivation is as certain as its premise. The premise is the part nobody has derived.
