1.2. The finding🔗

In brief. The law of marginal utility needs exactly one praxeological claim. That claim is not about action. This holds on both routes the authors wrote:

  • On Rothbard's route the claim is SwapDominant.

  • On Mises's route it is ServedInOrder.

A third reading trades it for a claim about action (ActsInOrder). That reading is taken up at the end of this part.

Go back to the six horses. On Rothbard's route the claim (SwapDominant) is about what the man would do. For each set of horses he might have, which wants would he serve? The answer is a plan. It is not a choice he makes.

Rothbard's own premise packs two claims into one sentence (MES p. 24):

  • A man acts with the means he has.

  • The wants he serves are "the most urgent of the not yet satisfied wants".

Only the second does any work here (SwapDominant). It does that work in the subjunctive. So does his own argument. It holds six horses up against five. It asks which want "the larger stock would have satisfied" (p. 25). No single real act can answer that.

The subjunctive alone is not the trouble. It is the obvious complaint, and it fails. Reasoning about stocks the man does not hold is Rothbard's own practice:

  • He builds the law upward, from one horse to two to three.

  • He takes it downward, from six horses to five.

  • He says outright that holding none of a good "does not affect the principle" (p. 32).

What he refuses on p. 33 is something else. It is a scale for a wholly different endowment: (3X, 4Y, 2Z) against (6X, 8Y, 5Z). Inside the stock the man actually has, he allows "adding and subtracting from stock". So his line is drawn around the stock in hand. It is not drawn at some distance from it. He steps one unit at a time. Stepping that way reaches every smaller set of horses there is. The formal claim (SwapDominant) reaches no further than he does.

The trouble is the tense. Rothbard restricts value scales in two phrases (p. 33):

  • "value scales do not exist in a void apart from the concrete choices of action"

  • "there is no need for him to formulate hypothetical value scales"

His man needs no scale in advance. The scale shows up choice by concrete choice, as each choice is made. The plan is different. It is a single standing object. It answers for every set of horses at once, before any of the choosing. That is precisely the scale in advance that Rothbard says is not needed. So the complaint is not that we asked what he would do with five horses. It is that we gave him a settled answer for every set at once. No act of his settles it.

The exemption. He saw a difficulty here. He exempted this law from the restriction by name. He did it on that same page, in a subordinate clause, with no argument attached. No one can predict with certainty the course of a man's choices, he says, "except that they will follow the law of marginal utility, which was deduced from the axiom of action".

Read the clause slowly, because everything turns on it. Before a man makes his choices, Rothbard will say only one thing about them with certainty: they will obey the law of marginal utility. Saying so is exactly what lets him hand the man a plan. The plan already answers for every smaller set of horses, as well as for the stock in hand. But the law gets proved from that very plan. The warrant for the premise is the conclusion.

What the machine enforces. Nothing in the Lean runs backwards. The derivation goes one way, from the claim to the law. The claim is never proved anywhere. It is handed in. The build passes. It would pass however the claim had been arrived at.

What a proof assistant enforces is narrower and stranger than it first looks. It enforces three things about the assumption:

  • It is stated.

  • It is stated in full.

  • It is spent in the open, where a reader can see what it cost.

It cannot ask whether Rothbard was entitled to assume it. Nothing can. That question took a reader. The reader held the stated claim in one hand and Rothbard's own rule about value scales in the other. And the question could be asked at all only because the encoding refused to let the claim stay unsaid.

This is not an objection from outside. The rule is Rothbard's. So is the exemption. The claim is Rothbard's in doctrine. Its one-swap form is ours.

The second demand: units. The same plan has to carry a second thing. It sits in a question usually discussed on its own. The law is expected to founder on units:

  • To state the law by the size of a supply, you seem to need units the agent values equally.

  • Rothbard denies that anyone ever demonstrates indifference by acting.

Here the law does not founder. Indifference is assumed nowhere, and the law survives without it. What the size-based wording needs is weaker. The plan must not care which units the man has, only how many (plan.Homogeneous). With the six horses, any five would be put to the same uses. But that is one more thing the standing plan must already have settled. Rothbard does not deduce it from the axiom of action. He grounds it in his definition of a supply: units "equally capable of rendering the same service to the actor" (p. 23).

That definition does not reach it. This is checked, not argued (unitsAlike_not_entail_homogeneous). Equal serviceability is a fact about the horses (unitsAlike, a field of the stock). The wording needs a fact about the man's plan for them. Take one stable of two horses, each able to serve any want. It carries a plan that treats the two alike. It also carries a plan that does not. The stock cannot settle it, because the plan is a separate thing.

Rothbard's other half would settle it: the man "must have valued each horse or each cow identically" (p. 23). But he withdraws that on the next page. There, interchangeability "does not mean that the concrete units are actually valued equally" (p. 24). Taking it back is what keeps indifference out of the law. The cost is that the definition no longer carries the condition the law's wording needs.

Two demands. The encoding keeps them apart:

  • The one the law is derived from is a praxeological claim (SwapDominant, on Rothbard's route). It rides on the signature of every theorem that uses it. If it is false, the law is false.

  • The one its wording needs is a hypothesis of one form of the law (plan.Homogeneous). Where it does not hold, the law simply does not speak. It says nothing false. It says nothing at all.

Telling which of the two gave way in a particular case is exactly what an audit like this is for.

None of this makes the law wrong. The machine checked the reasoning. The conclusion follows. The proof uses only what the statement lists. One question is left, and it is a narrow one. May praxeology help itself to that standing plan?

The other routes. Rothbard's is not the only derivation.

  • Mises reaches the same law by a different argument. Rebuilt the same way, it turns out to rest on a claim of the same kind (ServedInOrder). Mises does not warrant it either. It is set out under Mises's route.

  • A third way reads the ladder as a history of what the man served as his stock grew. It spends a claim about what he actually does (ActsInOrder), and no plan at all. It reaches the law only on the scale he ranked by before the stock grew. It pays for that in a different coin. The part called The ladder as a history sets it out.
