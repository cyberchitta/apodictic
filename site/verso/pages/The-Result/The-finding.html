1.2. The finding🔗

Terms such as library, claim and condition are explained under The Result.

In brief. Rothbard states the law of marginal utility on p. 27 of Man, Economy, and State:

The greater the supply of a good, the lower the marginal utility; the smaller the supply, the higher the marginal utility.

The marginal utility is the least important end the stock serves: "This least important end fulfilled by the stock is known as … marginal utility" (p. 27). Rothbard says the law was "deduced from the axiom of action" (p. 33). So one would expect it to need only claims about what people do. On both routes the authors wrote, it needs exactly one praxeological claim. That claim is not about action. It is about a plan: what the man would do with every part of his stock, settled in advance. It is not about anything he does. Each route has its own such claim:

  • On Rothbard's route the claim is SwapDominant.

  • On Mises's route it is ServedInOrder.

A third reading trades it for a claim about action (ActsInOrder). That reading is taken up at the end of this part.

Go back to Rothbard's man with six horses (see The horses). On Rothbard's route the claim (SwapDominant) is about what the man would do. For each set of horses he might have, which wants would he serve? The answer is a plan. It is not a choice he makes.

The claim ties the plan to his ranking. A bundle is a set of wants. Take any set of his horses and the bundle the plan would serve with it. Make any single swap: trade any want served for any want a horse could serve, by his lights, that the plan leaves out. The claim says he prefers the bundle the plan would serve to the bundle after the swap.

Rothbard's own premise packs two claims into one sentence (MES p. 24):

  • A man acts with the means he has.

  • The wants he serves are "the most urgent of the not yet satisfied wants".

Only the second does any work here. The claim (SwapDominant) keeps it and drops the first. It states it in the subjunctive: over the wants he would serve. So does Rothbard's own argument. It holds six horses up against five. It asks which want "the larger stock would have satisfied" (p. 25). No single real act can answer that.

The subjunctive alone is not the trouble. The plan has a trouble, and the next paragraphs locate it. One might complain that the claim speaks of stocks the man does not hold. That is the obvious complaint, and it fails. Reasoning about stocks the man does not hold is Rothbard's own practice:

  • He builds the law upward, from one horse to two to three.

  • He takes it downward, from six horses to five.

  • He says outright that holding none of a good "does not affect the principle" (p. 32).

What he refuses on p. 33 is something else. It is a hypothetical value scale for a wholly different endowment. His example has several goods, X, Y and Z. The man holds three units of X, four of Y and two of Z: (3X, 4Y, 2Z). The refused scale is for (6X, 8Y, 5Z). What he allows is different: his choices "for adding and subtracting from stock take place in this region". The region is that of the stock he has. So the line between what he allows and what he refuses is drawn around the stock in hand. It is not drawn at some distance from it. Each of his own steps adds or takes away one unit of the stock held at that point. His upward build is made of such steps: the man "successively finds one horse, then a second, then a third" (p. 24). Stepping down one unit at a time from six horses reaches every set of horses cut from those six. The formal claim (SwapDominant) speaks only of those sets. It reaches no further than he does.

The trouble is timing. What matters is when the answer is settled. Rothbard restricts value scales in two phrases (p. 33):

  • "value scales do not exist in a void apart from the concrete choices of action"

  • "there is no need for him to formulate hypothetical value scales"

His man needs no scale in advance. The scale shows up choice by concrete choice, as each choice is made. The plan is different. It is a single standing object. It answers for every set of horses at once, before any of the choosing. That is precisely the scale in advance that Rothbard says is not needed. More than that, he says scales "do not exist in a void apart from the concrete choices of action". The plan is a scale of the kind he denies. It stands apart from the choices. So the complaint is not that we asked what he would do with five horses. It is that we gave him a settled answer for every set at once. No act of his settles it.

The exemption. The law itself says something about the man's choices before they are made. Rothbard saw a difficulty here. He exempted this law by name. He did it on that same page, in a subordinate clause, with no argument attached. No one can predict with certainty the course of a man's choices, he says, "except that they will follow the law of marginal utility, which was deduced from the axiom of action".

Read the clause slowly, because everything turns on it. Before a man makes his choices, Rothbard will say only one thing about them with certainty: they will obey the law of marginal utility. The plan, too, says in advance what the man would choose. So saying this is what lets him hand the man a standing plan, one that answers for every smaller set of horses as well as for the stock in hand. But the law gets proved from that very plan. The warrant for the premise is the conclusion.

What the machine enforces. The circle is in the warrant, not in the proof. Nothing in the Lean is circular. The derivation goes one way, from the claim to the law. The claim is never proved anywhere. It is handed in. The build passes. It would pass however the claim had been arrived at.

What a proof assistant enforces is narrower and stranger than it first looks. It enforces three things about the assumption:

  • It is stated.

  • It is stated in full.

  • It is spent in the open, where a reader can see what it cost.

It cannot ask whether Rothbard was entitled to assume it. No machine check can. That question took a reader. The reader held the stated claim in one hand and Rothbard's own rule about value scales in the other. And the question could be asked at all only because the encoding refused to let the claim stay unsaid.

This is not an objection from outside. The rule is Rothbard's. So is the exemption. The claim is Rothbard's in doctrine. Its one-swap form is this document's encoding, not Rothbard's.

The second demand: units. The same plan has to carry a second thing. That thing belongs to a question usually discussed apart from this one: the question of units. The law is expected to founder on them:

  • To state the law by the size of a supply, you seem to need units the agent values equally.

  • Rothbard denies that anyone ever demonstrates indifference by acting.

Here the law does not founder. Indifference is assumed nowhere, and the law survives without it. The library states the law in two forms (see The theorems):

  • By the size of a supply, as Rothbard words it (marginal_utility).

  • Along a chain of named units, one added at a time (marginal_utility_chain).

The chain form needs no interchangeability of units, because it names which units are involved. What the size-based wording needs is weaker than indifference. The plan must not care which units the man has, only how many (plan.Homogeneous). With the six horses, any five would be put to the same uses. But that is one more thing the standing plan must already have settled. Rothbard does not deduce from the axiom of action that the plan cares only how many. He grounds it in his definition of a supply: units "equally capable of rendering the same service to the actor" (p. 23).

That definition does not deliver it. Equal serviceability of the units does not give a plan that cares only how many (plan.Homogeneous). This is checked, not argued: a theorem in the library proves it (unitsAlike_not_entail_homogeneous). Equal serviceability is a fact about the horses (unitsAlike, a condition carried by the stock). The wording needs a fact about the man's plan for them. Take one stable of two horses, each able to serve any want. It carries a plan that treats the two alike. It also carries a plan that does not. The stock cannot settle it, because the plan is a separate thing.

His definition of a supply has a second half, about valuation. That half would settle it: the man "must have valued each horse or each cow identically" (p. 23). But he withdraws that on the next page. There, interchangeability "does not mean that the concrete units are actually valued equally" (p. 24). Valuing each horse identically would be indifference among them. So taking it back is what keeps indifference out of the law. The cost is that the definition no longer carries the condition the law's wording needs.

Two demands. The encoding keeps them apart:

  • The one the law is derived from is a praxeological claim (SwapDominant, on Rothbard's route). It rides on the signature of every theorem that uses it. If it is false, praxeology has asserted something false, and the law loses its proof on this route.

  • The one its wording needs is a condition of the size form of the law (plan.Homogeneous). Where it does not hold, the law simply does not speak. It says nothing false. It says nothing at all.

Telling which of the two gave way in a particular case is exactly what an audit like this is for.

None of this makes the law wrong. The machine checked the reasoning. The conclusion follows. The proof uses only what the statement lists. One question is left, and it is a narrow one. May praxeology help itself to that standing plan? Rothbard's own warrant for it is circular, as shown above. Whether praxeology has another warrant, this page does not settle.

The other routes. Rothbard's is not the only derivation.

  • Mises reaches the same law by a different argument. Rebuilt the same way, it turns out to rest on a claim of the same kind (ServedInOrder). Mises does not warrant it either. It is set out under Mises's route.

  • A third way reads the ladder as a history. The ladder is the law built upward: one horse, then a second, then a third. As a history it is about what the man served as his stock grew. It spends a claim about what he actually does (ActsInOrder), and no plan at all. It proves the ladder only on the scale he ranked by before the stock grew. On that scale it is a short theorem. It restates acting in order about a want the next horse happens to serve. It also needs two conditions. Any two wants the good can serve were ranked at the earlier moment, one way or the other (earlier.ComparableServiceable). He learned no new use for the good between the two moments (earlier.NoNewUses later). On the later scale the ladder does not follow from acting in order, even granted at both moments (Model.later_judged_ladder_fails). It pays for doing without a plan in a different coin. The first two routes rest on a plan their authors refuse. This one needs a man who learned no new use for the good between the two acts. Neither author addresses that condition. Nothing in the texts grants it, and nothing refuses it. The part called The ladder as a history sets it out.
