1.11. Exchange🔗

Terms such as library, docstring, claim, condition, linter and frame are explained under The Result.

The law of marginal utility is one target. Exchange is the second. It was chosen for what it can diagnose. The tradition leans on few conclusions more than this one: both parties to a voluntary exchange benefit. Read as a statement about what each party expects when he trades, it is hard to fault. Read as a statement about how the trade turns out, it plainly misses some real trades. The buyer may have been mistaken about what he bought. Or he may regret it. So there are two questions. Which reading do Rothbard's words commit him to? And where the second reading misses, which assumption fails?

Rothbard states the doctrine in one paragraph (MES, p. 85). The paragraph says four things:

  • Why people trade: "both people make it because they expect that it will benefit them; otherwise they would not have agreed to the exchange."

  • When they can. The two goods must "have reverse valuations on the respective value scales of the two parties". That is, each ranks the other's good above his own. This must hold "before making the exchange".

  • What they must know: "each of the parties knows of the existence of the other and the goods that he possesses."

  • The result: "After the exchange is made, both A and B have shifted to a higher position on their respective value scales."

The paragraph also confines itself to "actions that are purely voluntary".

The bridge. "Otherwise they would not have agreed" infers what a man prefers from what he does. That is demonstrated preference (DemonstratedPreference). Its docstring is given in full under The claims. The law of marginal utility never needed it. Exchange cannot do without it. Every exchange theorem below runs back from what was done to what was preferred, and each one carries the bridge. Three things mark how it is stated:

  • It is stated of one trade, not of action in general.

  • It ranks whole holdings. One is everything the man owns with one good. The other is everything he owns with the other good. That is what a trade chooses between.

  • It counts each holding by what the man believes its goods can do. It takes his beliefs at the moment of trading.

How goods count. The third point above is how goods enter at all. Rothbard sets the value scales out as a table. It has two columns, one per party. The columns rank goods: A's column puts "(Good Y)" first and "Good X" second. "Parentheses around the good indicate that the party does not have it in his stock" (p. 85). The library ranks ends, not goods. So a good counts here for the ends the man believes it can serve (ActionFrame.ServedBy). "A values the typewriter above the vase" is read as "A prefers what he believes the typewriter can do to what he believes the vase can do". No doctrine of how goods take their value from ends is assumed.

But counting a good this way is a choice. It has two consequences:

  • A good counts for every end he believes it could serve. It does not count only for the one it would be put to. The definition (ActionFrame.ServedBy) asks only what he believes a good can serve.

  • Two goods that serve the same end count for it once.

Rothbard's case is single goods with separate uses. For those, counting by goods and counting by ends agree. The substitutes condition below (Trade.KeptServesOtherEnds) says that nothing he keeps is believed to serve an end either traded good serves. Where the two counts part, it is the one that fails. Trade and Exchange leave out any condition no proof uses. Five such conditions are listed after the theorems. The definitions:

One party's side of a trade. At time, agent gives up the good gives. He receives gets. He keeps kept.

refusal is what the agent would have been left with had he refused. It is a bundle of ends. In a voluntary trade it is simply his holding before the trade (Trade.Voluntary). Under a threat it is worse. It is a field because the act alone does not fix it. The same handing-over is a trade or a robbery. Which one it is depends on what refusing would have cost.

Shape: our-reconstruction. That is, the choice of fields that set out a trade is ours. kept is read as the rest of the holding. Rothbard assumes the two goods differ ("we implicitly assumed that it must be two different goods that are being exchanged", MES p. 95). For him it is a condition for an exchange to take place. No theorem in the library uses that either. So a Trade does not require it.

Reverse valuations follow, given a claim and three conditions per party. Take Rothbard's own case: "If A has a vase and B a typewriter, if each knows of the other's asset, and if A values the typewriter more highly, and B values the vase more highly, there will be an exchange" (p. 86). Rothbard runs from the valuations to the exchange. The theorem runs back, from the exchange to the valuations. Say the exchange is made, and the claim and conditions below hold. The theorem (MutualBenefit.reverse_valuations) concludes the following. A ranks the typewriter above the vase. B ranks the vase above the typewriter. Each ranks on his own scale, by his own beliefs, at the time of his trade. Put generally, each party ranks the good he got above the good he gave:

The manifest lists what the theorem takes, sorted by kind (see The manifest). Its last group is the conditions carried by the vocabulary. It is taken up below. The rest is one claim and three conditions per party. Rothbard's table shows none of the conditions. Here they are for A. B's are the same, with the goods swapped. Each can fail.

The claim is the bridge (DemonstratedPreference). It fails if A traded against his own ranking at the time.

The first condition is that the trade was voluntary (Trade.Voluntary). It fails if A handed over the vase under a threat. The bridge holds of a robbery too. The victim prefers handing over his wallet to what refusing would cost him. What it then shows is a ranking of his wallet against the threat. It does not rank his wallet against anything he got. So a trade records what refusing would have left the man with, alongside the goods. A trade is voluntary when refusing would have left him with exactly what he held before the trade. Rothbard rests the inference on exactly this: "The only reason we know that A and B benefit from an exchange is that they voluntarily make the exchange" (p. 1239).

🔗def
Apodictic.Trade.Voluntary {praxis : ActionFrame}
  (trade : Trade praxis) : Prop
Apodictic.Trade.Voluntary
  {praxis : ActionFrame}
  (trade : Trade praxis) : Prop

Voluntary — a condition on the situation. Refusing would have left the agent with exactly what he held. Nobody made refusal cost more. Rothbard confines the analysis of exchange to "actions that are purely voluntary" (MES p. 85). He rests the inference to benefit on voluntariness: "The only reason we know that A and B benefit from an exchange is that they voluntarily make the exchange" (p. 1239).

The other two conditions are what it costs to talk about two goods at all. The trade ranks whole holdings. The table ranks two goods. To get from one to the other, the rest of what the man owns must stay out of it. Rothbard's table says nothing of what else A owns. The theorem adds two conditions for that. They fail in two opposite ways:

  • A preference between the two holdings carries down to the two goods (Trade.SeparableFromRest). It fails for complements, goods that are worth more together. A left shoe is worth little without the right one.

  • Nothing he keeps is believed to serve an end either traded good serves (Trade.KeptServesOtherEnds). It fails for substitutes. Take a man with two umbrellas who trades one away. He gives up nothing the other cannot do. So the trade shows no ranking of the umbrella at all.

Rothbard's case is "unique goods with a supply of one unit" (p. 86). That phrase assumes the substitutes condition (Trade.KeptServesOtherEnds). It does not touch complements. A unique left shoe is still half a pair.

The first docstring below compares separability with independence of uses (IndependentUses). That is a condition the law of marginal utility carries, explained under The conditions.

🔗def
Apodictic.Trade.SeparableFromRest {praxis : ActionFrame}
  (trade : Trade praxis) : Prop
Apodictic.Trade.SeparableFromRest
  {praxis : ActionFrame}
  (trade : Trade praxis) : Prop

Separable from what is kept — a condition on the situation, NOT a universal claim. Take two holdings that differ in one part only. If the agent prefers one holding to the other, he prefers its part to the other's. It speaks only of holdings whose rest is what this agent keeps in this trade. It speaks only of parts that share no end with the kept goods. So it reaches the two traded goods only if their ends share none with the kept goods' ends. The substitutes condition (Trade.KeptServesOtherEnds) says exactly that.

It is like independence of uses (IndependentUses). That condition says a preference between two bundles that differ in one end carries down to those two ends. The rest is any set, and the two ends lie outside it. This one says it of two parts that are sets of ends, with the rest fixed to one trade's holding. Nothing is said about any other holding the agent might rank. It fails for complements. A man who keeps a right shoe may rank (right shoe + left shoe) above (right shoe + hat). He may still rank a hat above a left shoe on its own. The left shoe's worth is in the pair. Rothbard's p. 85 scales list the two goods alone. They never mention the rest of what the traders own. This condition is what that omission costs.

🔗def

  {praxis : ActionFrame}

The kept goods serve other ends than the traded goods — a condition on the situation, at the trade's time. Nothing the agent keeps is believed to serve an end either traded good serves. It fails for substitutes. Take an agent with two umbrellas who trades one away. The umbrella he keeps serves every end the traded one serves. Two goods that serve one end count for it once. So he gives up no end, and he demonstrates no ranking of the umbrella at all. It is the scope of "unique goods" (MES p. 86) made explicit.

What does no work. Knowledge of the other party is a condition for a trade to happen. Every theorem here starts from a trade that did happen. So knowledge does no work. It is not among the theorems' hypotheses, the claims and conditions a theorem takes. The unused-argument linter reports any hypothesis no proof uses, and a report fails the build. If knowledge were added as a hypothesis, the linter would report it.

"Shifted to a higher position" — on which scale? The same question came up under The ladder as a history. Is the position judged on the scale of the moment he acted, or on a later one? Here too there are those two readings. The question is settled differently here. The reading under The ladder as a history did not assume a constant scale, either as a claim or as a condition. Here constancy enters as a condition, for one pair of holdings only. It enters because the later-judged result needs it. Without it that result fails, as a model below shows.

Judged on the scale and the beliefs of the moment of trading, Rothbard's result is a theorem (MutualBenefit.better_off_judged_at_trade). It is a short one: the bridge plus voluntariness. On that reading, "benefit" is demonstrated preference in a voluntary trade, and nothing more.

Better off, judged at the trade — the holding after ranks above the holding before. The ranking is on the scale and beliefs of the moment of trading. This is Rothbard's "shifted to a higher position", read on the scale the traders had "before making the exchange".

Judged afterwards, it is a second theorem (MutualBenefit.better_off_judged_later). Take the vase and the typewriter again. The conclusion is about A at some later moment. On his later scale, he prefers his holding with the typewriter to his holding with the vase. Both holdings are counted by what he then believes. The theorem takes one claim and three conditions. Each can fail:

  • The bridge (DemonstratedPreference). It fails if A traded against his own ranking at the time.

  • The trade was voluntary (Trade.Voluntary). It fails if A handed over the vase under a threat.

  • His beliefs about the goods hold (Trade.BeliefsHold). Later, every good in the trade is believed to serve exactly what it was believed to serve when the trade was made. It fails if A comes to believe the typewriter does nothing. Rothbard's paragraph does not say either way. The theorem adds it as a condition.

  • His ranking of the two holdings holds (Trade.RankingHolds). It fails if A comes to want the vase back. Rothbard's paragraph does not say either way. The theorem adds it as a condition.

Judging afterwards needs the last two. Judging at the trade does without them. Each answers to one way the later result fails. The beliefs condition fails on a change of belief. The ranking condition fails on regret. The theorem and its manifest come first, then the two conditions:

It fails when the agent learns the good he got does less than he thought. That may come through his own error, or through deception. The ranking of the two holdings holds at later — a condition on the situation. Say that when trading, the agent ranked the holding he chose above the one he gave up. Then he still does. It fails when he changes his mind. That is regret. Only this one pair is constrained. Nothing is said of the rest of his scale.

Both are needed. A small made-up model shows that each is. The model is a frame, in the library's word. It is Rothbard's vase and typewriter, in two variants:

  • In one, A comes to believe the typewriter does nothing. His beliefs about it change. His ranking does not.

  • In the other, A comes to want the vase back. His beliefs stay. His ranking turns.

In each, A's trade is voluntary. It demonstrates what the bridge says. In neither is A better off by his later lights.

Fraud. Rothbard would not count the defrauded buyer against him. For him a deceived party has not traded voluntarily: "Since the exchange has been made falsely, the actual form of which might not have been contracted had the other party not been deceived, this is not an example of voluntary exchange, but of one-sided theft" (p. 184). So his "voluntary" does part of the work the beliefs condition (Trade.BeliefsHold) does here. It is the part where someone else changed what the buyer believed. Voluntariness here (Trade.Voluntary) asks only what refusing would have cost. It cannot see deception. The cut here is finer. Take a buyer who was deceived, and a buyer who was simply mistaken. Both fail the beliefs condition (Trade.BeliefsHold). Only the first is outside Rothbard's "voluntary". So only the second is a case his paragraph covers. He is the mistaken buyer of the opening. The opening's second reading, of how the trade turns out, misses him. Here the condition that fails for him has a name: the beliefs condition (Trade.BeliefsHold).

The carried conditions. The first manifest lists two fields of the exchange:

  • What A gives, B gets.

  • What B gives, A gets.

Neither says anything about the world. Suppose the good A hands over were not the good B receives. Then there would be no exchange to speak of. Whether a field says nothing about the world in this way is a judgement. The Lean cannot make it. It is made here as it is for the fields of a stock and a plan, under The vocabulary later in the document. The second manifest has no such group. Nothing is built into a trade as defined. The conditions its theorem needs are the named ones it lists.

Five conditions a reader might expect are in neither manifest:

  • A and B are two people.

  • The two trades are made at one moment.

  • The good given is not also kept.

  • The good received is not among the kept.

  • Two different goods change hands.

None is a field of Trade or of Exchange, because no proof uses it.

Two people and one moment do no work. The reason is that reverse_valuations applies a one-trade result (ranks_received_above_given) to each side. (Each party ranks what he got above what he gave.) All that ties the sides together is the two fields of an exchange: what A gives, B gets, and what B gives, A gets.

The next two are what makes kept the rest of a man's holding: neither the good given nor the good received is among the kept. A good both traded and kept is no trade in the ordinary sense. But a Trade does not exclude it. The substitutes condition (Trade.KeptServesOtherEnds) forbids a kept good to share any end with a traded good. So it excludes the case, unless the good counted twice is believed to serve no end at all. reverse_valuations carries that condition. The two better_off theorems do not, and nothing in them excludes the case. So in them it can arise two ways:

  • The good given is also kept. Then they say only that he prefers his holding with the received good to his holding without it. The given good is in both holdings, so they show no ranking of it.

  • The good received was already kept. Then they say only that he prefers his holding without the given good to his holding with it. The received good is in both holdings, so they show no ranking of it.

The fifth condition, two different goods, takes the next paragraph.

Rothbard states the two-different-goods condition himself: "In describing the conditions that must obtain for interpersonal exchange to take place (such as reverse valuations), we implicitly assumed that it must be two different goods that are being exchanged" (p. 95). For him it is a condition for an exchange to take place. So it is like knowledge of the other party, above. Every theorem here starts from a trade that did happen. So, like knowledge, it does no work in any proof. But leaving it out is not idle. It lets in the man who trades a good for itself. Of him the bridge, with voluntariness, says that he prefers his holding to itself. Prefers is meant as strict. But the library nowhere assumes that nothing is preferred to itself. That follows only from a claim of its own, asymmetry of preference (AsymmetricPreference, under The claims). The exchange theorems do not carry it. So as encoded nothing keeps him out. Assume the bridge of his trade, and the theorems still apply to him. They say only that he ranks a good, or a holding, above itself. Add asymmetry (AsymmetricPreference), and the bridge is false of his voluntary trade. Every theorem here about what a trader prefers asks for the bridge and for voluntariness. So with asymmetry added, none of them applies to him.

Where the tradition stands. For Rothbard the bridge cannot fail. The scale is read off the act: "We deduce the existence of a specific value scale on the basis of the real act" (p. 260). On that reading, an act against one's own ranking is not a possibility. Here the ranking exists apart from the act. So the bridge constrains it. Its status as a claim is our reconstruction and not his. Nor does the library assume, anywhere, that a man's scale stays put. The reasons are under The ladder as a history. What the later-judged benefit carries is narrower. It is a condition that his ranking of these two holdings did not turn (Trade.RankingHolds). Where it fails, the theorem is silent.

What a failure would point at. Two of the conclusions name different places to look: reverse valuations (MutualBenefit.reverse_valuations) and the later-judged benefit (MutualBenefit.better_off_judged_later). The at-trade result (MutualBenefit.better_off_judged_at_trade) takes only the bridge and voluntariness. Say a man did not rank the good he got above the good he gave. Then look for one of these:

  • The trade was not voluntary (Trade.Voluntary).

  • The goods were complements of something he kept (Trade.SeparableFromRest).

  • The goods were substitutes for something he kept (Trade.KeptServesOtherEnds).

  • He traded against his own ranking at the time. Then the bridge itself failed (DemonstratedPreference).

Say instead he is not better off afterwards. Then look for one of these:

  • The trade was not voluntary (Trade.Voluntary).

  • His beliefs about a good changed (Trade.BeliefsHold). That is error, or deception. Rothbard already counts deception as not voluntary.

  • His ranking of the two holdings turned (Trade.RankingHolds). That is regret.

  • The bridge failed (DemonstratedPreference).

On each list only the bridge is a claim. The rest are conditions. Where they fail, the theorems are silent, not wrong. The defence "the conditions did not hold" now has a list to check against.
