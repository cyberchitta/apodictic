1.4. The claims🔗

Terms such as library, docstring, claim, condition, linter, frame and vocabulary are explained under The Result.

The library contains no axiom. In Lean, an axiom can be used by any proof without appearing in the statement of the theorem it proves. Only a separate report shows it: the last line of each manifest. So instead, every praxeological claim is written as a structure. A theorem that needs a claim takes it as a named assumption. So to see what a theorem rests on, you read its statement.

A theorem's statement thus lists the claims it rests on. That list cannot be padded either. Take, say, a claim about action that the proof never touches. A theorem could list it only to look better grounded. #lint only unusedArguments runs on every build. It fails the build when a listed assumption does no work. So the list is held from both sides:

  • Lean rejects a theorem that leaves a premise off. Without the premise, the proof does not check.

  • The linter rejects a theorem that lists a premise it never uses.

That is the reason to do this on a machine. The hold is not perfect. Where it gives is set out under The manifest.

Below, a theorem is said to spend a claim when its proof uses it.

Each claim records three things:

  • Source: a citation, or "tacit". MES is Rothbard's Man, Economy, and State. Citations of Mises and Rothbard are to the Mises Institute editions.

  • Status: a verdict, or two, from the three listed next.

  • Does not say: the stronger nearby claims it deliberately avoids.

The three verdicts are these:

  • explicit-in-tradition: Rothbard or Mises say it.

  • suppressed-premise: they use it without saying it.

  • our-reconstruction: a decision the tradition never faced.

A Status may give two verdicts. The first is for the doctrine: what the tradition holds. The second is for the claim as written here, or for a named part of it, such as its form. So "explicit-in-tradition as doctrine; our-reconstruction as a claim" says this: the tradition states the doctrine, and the claim as it stands here is ours. Where there are two, the Status says which part is ours.

Two rules govern the list:

  • A claim is added only when some theorem first needs it. So every claim here is one that some theorem actually uses.

  • Only claims meant to hold always belong here. A condition says when a theorem applies. Where it fails, the theorem says nothing. It is not a claim. A theorem takes it as a hypothesis of its own, or finds it in a field of the vocabulary (see The Result). The docstrings below call such a condition either a hypothesis or a condition. The two words mean the same there. The conditions have a part of their own, The conditions.

The claims speak of a few things from the vocabulary:

  • The stock (Stock): the units of one good that a man holds at one time, and the ends the good can serve, by his lights.

  • The plan (AllocationPlan), for a stock: for any set of its units he might have, which ends he would serve with exactly those units. It is settled in advance.

  • The preference (Prefers): his ranking, at a given time, of one bundle of ends above another. A bundle is a set of ends. Nothing more is built into the ranking. A theorem that needs more takes it as a claim, such as the third below, or as a condition. The same ranking of single ends is PrefersEnd.

  • Times have names. now is the present time of a claim. It is a variable: any time can play that part.

Take a claim about every plan the man might have. From any one plan a neighbour can be defined: the same plan, changed by one swap at one set of units. Such a neighbour can break the claim. So a claim of that form is refutable by construction. Hence a rule of method: each claim about a plan is made of the one plan a theorem is handed, never of every plan. The rule is set out under The other findings.

A dotted name, such as Apodictic.Model.Impulse, is a name in the library. Names under Apodictic.Model are in Consistency.lean: the small frames and the theorems about them.

There are ten. The first three are these:

  • The first is the premise of Rothbard's derivation (SwapDominant). On his route it is the only claim the law of marginal utility needs. The theorem that derives the law there is marginal_utility.

  • The second is the premise of Mises's derivation, reconstructed in the same vocabulary (ServedInOrder). Mises argues for the law by a dilemma. The dilemma does not deliver this premise. A small frame meets everything the dilemma establishes, and breaks the law (Model.recurs_does_not_deliver_ladder). Both are set out under Mises's route.

  • The third is asymmetry of preference (AsymmetricPreference). If he prefers one bundle to another, he does not prefer the second to the first. Neither derivation spends it. The theorem relating the two routes does (Contrast.rothbard_entails_servedInOrder, under Mises's route). Four theorems under Interest also spend it. So does the rival-plan theorem (no_rival_swap_dominant, under The theorems). Take a plan that satisfies swap dominance, and a rival plan for the same stock. At one set of units on hand, the rival would serve what the first would serve there, with one change. One end the first would serve is swapped out. In its place is an end the good can serve that the first would leave out there. Given asymmetry, the theorem says the rival does not also satisfy swap dominance.

The next two are about what a man actually does, not what he would do:

  • The fourth is the premise of the ladder read as a history (ActsInOrder). The ladder is the law built upward: one horse, then a second, then a third, in Rothbard's example of a man's horses (see The horses). Read as a history, it is about what the man served as his stock grew. The docstring calls this the temporal reading. The claim runs from his ranking to his act. The theorem under The ladder as a history carries it (Temporal.ladder_judged_earlier).

  • The fifth runs the other way, from an act to his ranking. It is demonstrated preference (DemonstratedPreference). Rothbard states it of any action. Here it is stated of one trade. Exchange, The regression theorem and Interest carry it.

The next two are about time:

  • The sixth is time preference (TimePreference). A satisfaction is preferred sooner rather than later. One theorem under Time preference carries it (TimePreference.less_durable_preferred). So do the interest theorems that conclude a preference or a rate (PureInterest.present_sum_preferred, PureInterest.present_sum_preferred_to_less and the three loan theorems under Interest).

  • The seventh is its demonstration (DemonstratedTimePreference). It is stated of one act. Taking a satisfaction and forgoing the same satisfaction later shows a preference for the sooner. Like the fifth, it runs from an act to his ranking. It is set out under Time preference. Two theorems there carry this claim (TimePreference.consumption_reveals, TimePreference.regress).

The next two are about a medium of exchange. Both are premises of the regression theorem, under The regression theorem. Its argument runs back through a record of exchanges. The docstrings call that argument a regress. The seventh claim's docstring uses the word for a different argument, Mises's on time preference (see Time preference).

  • The eighth is appraisal from the past (AppraisalFromPast). Nobody appraises a good as a medium unless it changed hands on some earlier day.

  • The ninth is Mises's division of the demand for a medium into two (TwoPartialDemands). Sort the ends into those reached by passing a good on, and the rest. The sort leaves none over. Classical logic would grant that sort for free, by assuming every statement is true or false. That assumption is called excluded middle. The library's theorems outside Consistency.lean do without it: they are constructive. So the sort has to be stated, and this claim states it.

Where the sort is needed comes from the theorem's two forms. Mises states the theorem in the negative. Say nobody acquired the good, before a given day, for an end other than passing it on. Then on that day nobody appraises it as a medium. The positive form turns it round. Say the good is a medium on some day. Then on an earlier day, someone acquired it for an end other than passing it on. At each step back, the proof meets an acquirer and the end he wanted the good for. The positive form has to decide whether that end is onward or not. The negative form never has to decide. So the positive forms carry the ninth claim (Regression.direct_acquisition_before, Regression.narrow_acquisition_before, Regression.barter_origin, Regression.prior_money_relates_back). The negative forms do not. Two other theorems carry it too (Regression.partial_demands_exhaust, Regression.only_onward_without_direct).

The tenth is the interest theorems' own claim. It is about sums of money (MoreMoneyPreferredWhenSpent). Take two sums of money, one part of the other. Take the date he would spend them (spend). The claim says this. Say both sums are free to hold now: holding them costs him nothing. And say the two would buy different ends. Then, at that date, he prefers what the larger would buy to what the smaller would buy. The claim applies only where the two would buy different ends. It does not say that they do. A theorem that needs that takes a condition (BuysMoreThanItsParts): every strict part of a given sum would buy other ends than the whole does. The theorems that carry the claim also carry time preference (TimePreference). They are under Interest.

The manifests in each part list exactly which claims each theorem carries.

Swap dominance — the agent's plan beats every one-swap alternative to it.

Take any sub-stock of the units on hand. Take the ends the agent would serve with it. Now make one swap. Drop a served end. Put in its place an unserved one: an end the good could have served but the plan left out. The claim is that the agent prefers the bundle he would have served to the swapped bundle.

It is asserted of one given plan. It is subjunctive throughout. It speaks of sub-stocks the agent may not hold, and of swaps he does not make.

Source: Rothbard, MES, ch. 1, §5.B, pp. 24–27 (Mises Institute ed.): "action uses scarce means to satisfy the most urgent of the not yet satisfied wants" (p. 24). The counterfactual framing is Rothbard's own ("suppose ... faced with the necessity of giving up one horse"; "he gives up the least urgent of the wants which the larger stock would have satisfied", p. 25). It is backed by the reallocation argument (p. 27: "follows from the defined interchangeability of units and from disregard of past events"). Mises, Human Action, ch. VII.1.

Status: explicit-in-tradition as doctrine. Two things are our-reconstruction: the one-swap form, and the pulling apart of the two halves below.

Rothbard's premise fuses two claims in one sentence. One is that ACTION employs the means. The other is that the wants served are "the most urgent of the not yet satisfied wants" (MES p. 24). This claim keeps the ordering half. It states it subjunctively, over what the agent WOULD serve. The subjunctive is Rothbard's own. His derivation compares a stock of six with a stock of five. It asks which want "the larger stock would have satisfied" (p. 25). No single actual allocation delivers that comparison. The action half is left out of this claim. Nothing here derives it. Nothing here bridges an act to a preference.

A tension remains, and it is Rothbard's own. It is not that this claim reaches sub-stocks the agent does not hold. That reach is his (see Anything about units not on hand, below). It is that the claim attributes a STANDING disposition. The plan is one object, at one time. It assigns served ends to every sub-stock at once. That is a value scale formulated in advance of the choices it covers. It is the thing Rothbard says is not needed. He holds that praxeology "may deal with utilities only as deduced from the concrete actions of human beings" (MES p. 882 n. 8). Inside this very chapter he writes that "value scales do not exist in a void apart from the concrete choices of action". He introduces those words with "It must be reiterated". He adds that "there is no need for him to formulate hypothetical value scales" (MES p. 33). His actor needs no scale in advance. The scale is revealed choice by concrete choice as he goes. This claim gives him one up front.

Yet on that same page he exempts this law from the restriction. He does it in a subordinate clause, without argument. Choices cannot be predicted "except that they will follow the law of marginal utility, which was deduced from the axiom of action". So the one thing that may be said in advance about the choices is that they follow the law. That is what licenses attributing the standing plan this claim is asserted of. And that plan is the premise the law is derived from. The warrant for the premise is the conclusion.

The circle is in the WARRANT, not in the proof. Nothing in the Lean is circular. marginal_utility derives the law from this claim. This claim is asserted, never proved. What is circular is Rothbard's licence for asserting it.

It is asserted of ONE plan, not of every plan of that shape. That is not a hedge. Given one plan, a rival can always be defined: the same plan with one swap at one set of units. Given asymmetry, such a rival breaks any claim about every plan of that shape. Asserted of the single plan a theorem is handed, it breaks nothing. Whether that rival could also be swap-dominant is then a theorem (Apodictic.no_rival_swap_dominant), and it cannot. It is not a premise.

Within the region it reaches (bullet 4 below), this claim departs from Rothbard in INDEXING. It speaks of arbitrary sub-stocks where he speaks of sizes. The gap between the two is interchangeability. He assumes it throughout. This claim does not: interchangeability is the hypothesis Homogeneous. Stated without that assumption, the claim distinguishes cases he never had to tell apart.

Does not say:

  1. Anything about actual action. No bridge from action to preference is part of this claim.

  2. Anything about alternatives that differ by more than one swap.

  3. Anything about independence of uses. That is the theorems' hypothesis IndependentUses.

  4. Anything about units not on hand. The plan speaks only of sub-stocks of the units on hand (subStock ⊆ stock.units). Rothbard's own law reaches stocks the agent does not hold, in both directions from the one he holds. He builds the law upward over stocks the actor lacks: the second unit, then the third "added to a stock of two units" (MES pp. 23–24). He also takes it downward, six horses to five (p. 25). Holding none of a good is no obstacle either: "If the actor has no units of some goods in his possession, this does not affect the principle" (p. 32). The hypothetical scales he refuses on the next page are a different thing. They are scales for a wholly different multi-good endowment, (3X, 4Y, 2Z) against (6X, 8Y, 5Z). Inside the actual stock he permits "adding and subtracting" units of it (p. 33). The line between those scales and adding to or subtracting from the actual stock is anchored to the stock the agent has. It is not drawn at some distance from it. This claim reaches only downward. His single-unit steps (first horse, then second, then third), taken downward from a stock, reach every sub-stock of it, and quantifying over all of subStock ⊆ stock.units covers each of them. The upward step he also permits, adding a unit to the stock, it leaves out.

  5. Anything about interchangeability of units. That two sub-stocks of the same size would serve the same ends is the hypothesis Homogeneous. It is no part of this claim.

  6. That such a plan exists. That it is unique (Apodictic.no_rival_swap_dominant rules out only a rival one swap away).

Served in order of urgency — nothing serviceable and more urgent is passed over for something less urgent.

Say the good can serve an end. Say that end is more urgent than something the agent WOULD serve. Then he would serve it too. Like SwapDominant, it is subjunctive throughout. It is asserted of one given plan.

This is the premise of MISES's route to the law (Apodictic.Mises.ladder_from_order). SwapDominant is the premise of Rothbard's. The two are not equivalent:

  • Rothbard's premises entail this one, given asymmetry (Apodictic.Contrast.rothbard_entails_servedInOrder).

  • This one does not entail his (Apodictic.Model.servedInOrder_not_entail_swapDominant).

As reconstructed here, Mises's route asks less of the allocation than Rothbard's premises taken together: they entail it, given asymmetry, and it does not entail them. It asks more of the value scale: any two ends the good can serve are ranked (Stock.ComparableServiceable), which Rothbard's route never needs.

Source: Mises, Human Action, ch. VII, §1: "If the supply available increases from n–1 units to n units, the increment can be employed only for the removal of a want which is less urgent or less painful than the least urgent or least painful among all those wants which could be removed by means of the supply n–1". Then the bridge sentence the derivation turns on: "It is nothing else than the reverse of the statement that what satisfies more is preferred to what gives smaller satisfaction." Compare Mises ch. IV, §2. There the agreement of action with the value scale holds by interpretation. It constrains nothing: "Every action is always in perfect agreement with the scale of values or wants because these scales are nothing but an instrument for the interpretation of a man's acting." Rothbard states the doctrine in the same family: "action uses scarce means to satisfy the most urgent of the not yet satisfied wants" (MES p. 24).

Status: our-reconstruction. Mises argues for the law by a dilemma about whether action terminates: either there are intermediate stages before the state in which no further action is possible, or there are not. He does not argue from a stated ordering premise. This claim is what that argument needs to get from the dilemma to the law, and does not supply. The dilemma is machine-checked NOT to deliver it (Apodictic.Model.recurs_does_not_deliver_ladder). Mises also refutes the second horn, that there could be only one action. That refutation does not go through on the bare claim that there is action (Apodictic.Mises.ActionOccurs; Apodictic.Model.existence_does_not_refute_second_horn).

The warrant problem is not Rothbard's alone. Mises has it too. He restricts value scales exactly as Rothbard does: "These scales have no independent existence apart from the actual behavior of individuals" (ch. IV, §2). Then, in ch. VII, §1, he holds the openly counterfactual definition licit: "We do not transcend the sphere of praxeological reasoning in establishing the following definition". He gives a reason: "In order to attain this knowledge we do not need any physiological or psychological experience, knowledge, or reasoning. It follows necessarily from our assumptions that people act (choose) and that in the first case acting man has n units of a homogeneous supply and in the second case n–1 units." The reason takes the n–1 case as an assumption. It does not say what act would show the choice he would make there. And it is given for the definition, not for this claim. Rothbard exempts the law circularly. Mises assumes the counterfactual case. Neither warrants a counterfactual premise.

Does not say:

  1. Anything about bundles. That the served bundle beats each one-swap rival is SwapDominant. That is a different claim, of a different shape.

  2. That the ends a good can serve are comparable. That is the situational condition Stock.ComparableServiceable. The route that uses this claim needs it separately.

  3. Anything about actual action. No bridge from an act to a preference is part of this claim.

  4. Anything about units not on hand. It speaks of subStock ⊆ stock.units, the same region SwapDominant speaks of.

Asymmetry of preference — if one bundle is preferred to another, the other is not preferred to the first.

This is the "strict" reading of Prefers. Apodictic.Contrast.rothbard_entails_servedInOrder argues by contradiction from a preference running both ways. Without asymmetry there is no contradiction to draw. Apodictic.no_rival_swap_dominant spends it the same way. So do the loan theorems Apodictic.PureInterest.no_loan_at_zero_rate and Apodictic.PureInterest.no_loan_at_nonpositive_rate. Apodictic.PureInterest.struck_rate_positive uses it only by passing it to Apodictic.PureInterest.no_loan_at_nonpositive_rate, which its proof applies. Apodictic.PureInterest.buysMore_of_unrestricted uses it to rule out a bundle preferred to itself.

Source: Mises, Human Action, ch. II, §2: "The idea that A could at the same time be non-A or that to prefer A to B could at the same time be to prefer B to A is simply inconceivable and absurd to a human mind." Rothbard's value scales are built from acts of choice, and one act cannot demonstrate a preference in both directions. But that is an argument from the bridge (demonstrated preference), not a statement of his. And the bridge as stated here (DemonstratedPreference, of one trade) yields a preference in one direction only. Nozick's reconstruction (1977) treats it explicitly. That is a critic's formalization, not the tradition's own words.

Status: explicit-in-tradition. Mises states it as a law of thought, of preference at one time.

Does not say: not transitivity. Not totality. Not comparability of any particular pair. It is asserted of one agent at one time. It is asserted of the bundle-level relation. The end-level case follows from it, since PrefersEnd is Prefers on singletons.

Acts in order of urgency — nothing serviceable and more urgent is passed over for something less urgent, in what the agent ACTUALLY does with the stock he holds.

Say the agent serves an end. Say he prefers to it another end he believes the good can serve. Then he serves that end too. This is the actual-action twin of ServedInOrder. That claim is subjunctive: it is asserted of a plan over sub-stocks the agent may not hold. This one is asserted of one allocation of one stock at one time. The TEMPORAL reading of the law (Apodictic.Temporal) spends it in place of a standing plan.

Source: Rothbard, MES, ch. 1, §5.B, p. 24, in the passage that narrates the acquisition of horses one by one: "The first horse will fulfill the most urgent wants that a horse can serve; this follows from the universal fact that action uses scarce means to satisfy the most urgent of the not yet satisfied wants". Mises, Human Action, ch. IV, §2, states the same agreement and gives its ground: "Every action is always in perfect agreement with the scale of values or wants because these scales are nothing but an instrument for the interpretation of a man's acting."

Status: our-reconstruction. The words are Rothbard's. The claim is not quite his. Both authors deny that a value scale exists apart from the action it is read from. Mises: "The scale of value manifests itself only in real acting; it can be discerned only from the observation of real acting. It is therefore impermissible to contrast it with real acting and to use it as a yardstick for the appraisal of real actions" (ch. V, §4). Rothbard: "praxeology may deal with utilities only as deduced from the concrete actions of human beings" (p. 882). On their account the sentence holds of every act by interpretation. No act could contradict it. Here PrefersEnd is a ranking kept distinct from the allocation. So the claim constrains the act. Take an act that serves a lesser end while passing over a preferred end he believes the good can serve. That act refutes it. Only the ordering half of Rothbard's sentence is taken. Its "scarce means" half is not.

Does not say:

  1. Anything about sub-stocks the agent does not hold, or about any other time. In particular, nothing about how this allocation relates to one made at another time with a different stock. That relation, if any, is a theorem's to state and pay for.

  2. That action reveals preference. This claim runs from the ranking to the act. The bridge from an act to a preference (DemonstratedPreference) is stated of trades only. Allocations are not trades. A bridge that read the ranking off the act would make this claim hold by construction. That is the authors' reading above.

  3. That the ends a good can serve are comparable (Stock.ComparableServiceable). Nor anything about the order between two ends both served.

  4. That the scale at this time bears on the scale at any other (constancy). Mises denies it: "value judgments are not immutable" (ch. V, §4). The library does not assert it as a claim. As a condition it appears once, for one pair of holdings in one trade (Trade.RankingHolds). Where the temporal reading would need it, the library instead builds a frame in which the ladder fails on the later scale (Apodictic.Model.later_judged_ladder_fails).

  5. How much is served. Not that every unit is used. Not that as many ends are served as the stock allows. An agent who serves nothing satisfies it.

  6. Anything about ends the agent does not believe the good can serve. Passing those over is a matter of belief, not of this claim.

Demonstrated preference, in exchange — the agent of a trade prefers the holding he chose to what refusing would have left him with. He prefers it at the time of trading. The holding he chose is the set of ends its goods serve, by his beliefs at that time.

This is the bridge from what an agent DOES to the ranking Prefers. The vocabulary otherwise keeps the two apart. It is asserted of the trade handed to a theorem, not of every trade.

Source: Rothbard, MES, p. 307: "Any action demonstrates choice based on preference: preference for one alternative over others." p. 85, of exchange: "both people make it because they expect that it will benefit them; otherwise they would not have agreed to the exchange." p. 260: "We deduce the existence of a specific value scale on the basis of the real act". The doctrine is named "demonstrated preference" at p. 882, n. 8, citing Rothbard, Toward a Reconstruction of Utility and Welfare Economics (1956), pp. 224 ff.

Status: explicit-in-tradition as doctrine; our-reconstruction as a claim. For Rothbard the scale is DEDUCED from the act (p. 260). So the sentence cannot fail: it defines what the scale says. Here Prefers exists apart from the act. So the claim constrains it. A trade made against the agent's own ranking at that moment refutes it. A slip can be such a trade. So can a compulsion he would disown.

Does not say:

  1. That the agent is better off afterwards. The comparison is made with his beliefs and his scale AT THE TRADE. It does not touch what the holding does for him later, or how he ranks it later (Trade.BeliefsHold, Trade.RankingHolds).

  2. Anything about the two goods alone. It ranks whole holdings. The descent to "Y over X" is a theorem's to pay for (Trade.SeparableFromRest, Trade.KeptServesOtherEnds).

  3. That the trade was voluntary. Under a threat the claim still holds: the victim prefers handing over to what refusing would cost. It then demonstrates nothing about the goods. Trade.Voluntary is what makes refusal the old holding.

  4. Anything about other acts, other agents, or other times. It does not say that every act demonstrates preference.

Apodictic.TimePreference

Time preference — of two ends that are the same satisfaction, the agent, at the time now, prefers the one attained sooner. This holds provided the sooner one is not already past.

Source: Rothbard, MES, ch. 1, p. 15: "A fundamental and constant truth about human action is that man prefers his end to be achieved in the shortest possible time. Given the specific satisfaction, the sooner it arrives, the better." / "This is the universal fact of time preference." Mises, Human Action, ch. XVIII, §2: "Satisfaction of a want in the nearer future is, other things being equal, preferred to that in the farther distant future." / "Time preference is a categorial requisite of human action."

Status: explicit-in-tradition as doctrine; our-reconstruction as a claim. Rothbard fixes "the same satisfaction" by the satisfaction it gives. Ice in summer and ice in winter "are not the same, but different goods" (MES pp. 15–16, n. 15). Read that way, the sentence cannot fail: whatever is preferred later was a different good. Mises sets ice aside on another ground, as "the case of perishable goods". Winter ice is "a good that loses its specific usefulness even if not consumed". Summer ice is "another good which requires a different process of production" (Human Action, ch. XVIII, §2). Neither is a fact about the ranking. Here SameSatisfaction is supplied apart from Prefers. So the claim constrains the ranking. An agent who prefers the same satisfaction later refutes it. The reading on which it cannot fail is built as the rejected encoding (Apodictic.TimePreference.valuation_reading_is_free). Carried by Apodictic.TimePreference.less_durable_preferred and by the interest theorems in Apodictic.PureInterest.

Does not say:

  1. Anything about ends that are not the same satisfaction. "Other things being equal" is carried whole by SameSatisfaction. A perishable good gives a pair that is not the same satisfaction. So do two enjoyments that cannot be had together (Mises's Carmen and Hamlet). So does unequal certainty of getting it. The claim is silent on each. It is silent on anticipation too. A pleasure put off brings the looking forward with it, from now until it comes. A pain got over with spares the waiting for it. Either way, the later option holds a satisfaction that begins now, and the earlier does not. That is the literature's own account: savouring is "positive utility derived from anticipation of future consumption", dread "negative utility resulting from contemplation of the future" (Loewenstein, Economic Journal 1987, p. 667). Anticipation points at a failed condition only where it is shown apart from the choice it explains. Inferred from the choice to wait, it is the valuation reading again.

  2. Anything about bundles. Preferring a sooner bundle needs DatedFrame.LiftsOverRest.

  3. Anything about ends already past at now.

  4. That Before is transitive, irreflexive or total. The claim uses "sooner" only as a label on the pair.

  5. How MUCH sooner is better. No rate, no discounting.

  6. That the preference shows in any act. That bridge is DemonstratedTimePreference, of one act.

Apodictic.DemonstratedTimePreference {praxis : DatedFrame}

Apodictic.DemonstratedTimePreference
  {praxis : DatedFrame}
  (act : Action praxis.toActionFrame) :
  Prop

Demonstrated time preference — an act that takes a satisfaction and forgoes the same satisfaction later shows that the agent prefers the sooner, at the time of acting.

This is the bridge from an act to the ranking, for acts across dates. DemonstratedPreference is that bridge for trades. It is asserted of the act handed to a theorem, not of every act.

Source: Mises, Human Action, ch. XVIII, §2: "He who consumes a nonperishable good instead of postponing consumption for an indefinite later moment thereby reveals a higher valuation of present satisfaction". Rothbard, MES, p. 307: "Any action demonstrates choice based on preference: preference for one alternative over others."

Status: explicit-in-tradition. Mises states it, in the same paragraph as his regress. The regress runs on a premise it does not state: that an agent with no preference for the sooner postpones. This claim is that premise read the other way round. It is carried by Apodictic.TimePreference.consumption_reveals and by Mises's regress (Apodictic.TimePreference.regress).

Does not say:

  1. Anything about acts that forgo something other than the same satisfaction later. "Nonperishable" is carried by SameSatisfaction.

  2. That the chosen satisfaction is present, rather than merely sooner.

  3. Anything about other acts or other times.

Apodictic.AppraisalFromPast {praxis : MediumFrame}
  (history : History praxis) (m : praxis.Means) : Prop
Apodictic.AppraisalFromPast
  {praxis : MediumFrame}
  (history : History praxis)
  (m : praxis.Means) : Prop

Appraisal from the past, weak form — nobody appraises a good as a medium of exchange on a day unless that good changed hands, against anything, on some earlier day.

It is asserted of one record of exchanges and one good. "Appraises as a medium" is a belief that the good serves an onward end (MediumFrame.AppraisesAsMedium). So the claim is about what any prospective acquirer or holder can believe. It is not only about acquirers.

Source: Mises, Human Action, ch. XVII, §4: "he who considers acquiring or giving away money is, of course, first of all interested in its future purchasing power and the future structure of prices. But he cannot form a judgment about the future purchasing power of money otherwise than by looking at its configuration in the immediate past." Ch. XVII, §8: "A medium of exchange without a past is unthinkable." Ch. XVII, §1: "For all that is to be predicated of money is valid for every medium of exchange." Rothbard, MES p. 275: "Demand for a good as a medium of exchange must be predicated on a previously existing array of prices in terms of other goods." Rothbard, Power and Market (MES ed. p. 1021): a money must be one that "can be exchanged for a previously existing money".

Status: explicit-in-tradition as doctrine; the weak form is our-reconstruction. Mises states the premise exclusively. The judgment rests on the good's own price, from the immediate past, and on nothing else. This form keeps only that an exchange of the good came first. That exchange may be on any earlier day, and against any good, money included.

Does not say:

  1. That the earlier exchange was on the immediately preceding day.

  2. That the appraisal rests on that exchange, or on that exchange alone. Nor that pledges, edicts or cross rates play no part. The vocabulary has no relation for "rests on". See History.OwnPastPrice.

  3. That the earlier exchange was barter (History.FormedInBarter).

  4. That the earlier acquirer wanted the good for itself, for an end other than passing it on. From further conditions, the regression theorem's positive form derives something weaker: someone, on some earlier day, acquired it so (Apodictic.Regression.direct_acquisition_before).

  5. Anything about day 0. That nobody appraises the good as a medium on the first day of the record is a separate condition, History.BeginsBeforeMedium. It is the termination Mises asserts.

  6. That the record is complete. The claim is asserted of the record it is handed. Say an earlier exchange happened but is missing from that record. Then the claim cannot use that exchange.

Apodictic.TwoPartialDemands (praxis : MediumFrame) (m : praxis.Means) :
  Prop
Apodictic.TwoPartialDemands
  (praxis : MediumFrame)
  (m : praxis.Means) : Prop

Two partial demands — every end is either one reached by passing the good m on, or not. That is, the demand for a medium of exchange divides without remainder into the two demands Mises names.

It is asserted of one frame and one good. Its content is excluded middle for the one predicate Onward m: each end is onward for m or is not. Classical logic would grant that for free. The theorems that need the split are proved constructively and do not assume it, and the vocabulary cannot decide it. So where a theorem needs the split, the split is this claim.

Source: Mises, Human Action, ch. XVII, §4: "Thus the demand for a medium of exchange is the composite of two partial demands: the demand displayed by the intention to use it in consumption and production and that displayed by the intention to use it as a medium of exchange."

Status: explicit-in-tradition as doctrine. The reading of "composite" as an exhaustive sort of ends is our-reconstruction.

Does not say:

  1. That the non-onward part is consumption or production. Mises names them. This claim leaves the sort "not onward". The narrow reading is the separate condition History.OtherEmploymentsNarrow.

  2. That the two demands add up, or have sizes at all. No quantities.

  3. That an agent can tell which kind an end is. Nor that the sort can be computed. It says each end IS one or the other.

  4. That excluded middle holds for any other predicate.

Apodictic.MoreMoneyPreferredWhenSpent {praxis : MoneyFrame}
  {agent : praxis.Agent} {now : praxis.Time}
  {stock : Stock praxis.toActionFrame agent now}
  (plan : AllocationPlan stock) (spend : praxis.Time) : Prop
Apodictic.MoreMoneyPreferredWhenSpent
  {praxis : MoneyFrame}
  {agent : praxis.Agent}
  {now : praxis.Time}
  {stock :
    Stock praxis.toActionFrame agent now}
  (plan : AllocationPlan stock)
  (spend : praxis.Time) : Prop

More money is preferred when it is spent — take two sums of money, the one part of the other. Say both are free to hold, and they would buy different ends. Take spend, the date the agent would spend them. At that date he prefers what the larger would buy to what the smaller would buy.

It is asserted of one plan for one stock. It is asserted only of sums whose every unit is money and which cost nothing to hold. It is subjunctive, like the plans it is asserted of: it speaks of sums the agent may not hold. The plan says what he WOULD buy with a sum. He would buy it at spend, by his valuations then. The claim ranks the purchase there. Two further things are conditions, each on the theorem that needs it:

  • That he ranks it so at now too is the condition RankedAlikeNowOf.

  • That a sum would buy other ends than each of its strict parts is the condition BuysMoreThanItsParts. The claim itself applies only where the two sums differ in what they buy.

Source: Rothbard, MES p. 379: "[T]o both present money and future money there applies the general rule that more of a good will have greater utility than less of it." Mises, Human Action, ch. VII, §1: "what satisfies more is preferred to what gives smaller satisfaction".

Status: explicit-in-tradition as doctrine; the narrow form is our-reconstruction. Rothbard states a general rule, of every good. Known cases refute it as stated:

  • A holding taxed at more than its whole value makes more of a good worse than less. So, at small scale, does any storage or custody cost.

  • A sum beyond anything the agent would spend or hold it on would buy nothing more.

  • Take a man whose valuations change between now and the spending. He now prefers less cash at hand, because of what he would buy with it later (Apodictic.Model.Impulse).

So the claim is cut to money, to sums free to hold, to purchases that differ, and to the date of spending. The claim speaks only of such sums, at the date of spending. A theorem that applies it to a sum in hand takes the rest as named conditions on that sum: that every unit is money (MoneyFrame.Money), that every part of it is free to hold (FreeToHoldWithin), that it would buy other ends than each of its strict parts (BuysMoreThanItsParts), and that the ranking at spend holds now (RankedAlikeNowOf). Carried by Apodictic.PureInterest.present_sum_preferred_to_less and the loan theorems built on it.

Does not say:

  1. Anything about his valuations at any date but spend. That they agree with his valuations now is a condition, not the claim.

  2. That every sum in the plan is spent at the one date spend, or which date that is. spend is supplied with the plan.

  3. That an extra unit would always buy something else.

  4. Anything of goods that are not money, or of sums that cost something to hold.

  5. How much more is preferred. No rate.

  6. Anything of two sums neither of which is part of the other. That two sums of the same size would buy the same is the condition AllocationPlan.Homogeneous.

  7. That the agent's preferences chain. Transitivity is the condition ActionFrame.TransitiveOn, for three given bundles only.

  8. Anything about acts. It ranks what sums would buy. The bridge to a trade is DemonstratedPreference.
