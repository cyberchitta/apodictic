1.13. The regression theorem🔗

The regression theorem is the fourth target. It is Mises's account of where a medium of exchange can come from: "no good can be employed for the function of a medium of exchange which at the very beginning of its use for this purpose did not have exchange value on account of other employments" (Human Action, ch. XVII, §4). It was chosen because a case is pressed against it. Some media came into use inside a monetary economy, Bitcoin among them, and their first buyers paid money for them. What the theorem requires of such a medium depends on how its terms are read, and with the readings written separately in Lean, moving from one to another is a change of hypothesis that shows in a signature.

The theorem has two halves, and only one is here: the genetic half, about a medium's origin. The other, that today's purchasing power is formed from yesterday's, is about quantities, and nothing below has any. And the theorem is about any medium of exchange, not only money: "For all that is to be predicated of money is valid for every medium of exchange" (ch. XVII, §1).

A medium, by belief. Mises's medium of exchange is a good wanted "to keep it as a medium of exchange and to give it away at need in a later act of exchange" (ch. XVII, §4). The vocabulary grows by one bare relation to say so: an end is onward for a good when the man would reach it by giving that good itself away in a later trade.

Onward m e: e is an end the agent would reach by giving m itself away in a later trade. No properties.

A man appraises a good as a medium when he believes it serves some onward end. Two other definitions were available and neither is used. One calls a good a medium when its price is being appraised; then a medium would have a price behind it by the meaning of the word, and the regress would be true by definition. The other waits for an actual later trade; but a man who holds a good meaning to pass it on already demands it as a medium, whether or not the trade comes. So demand in anticipation counts.

A good, here, is a kind — gold, salt — and not a unit of one. The regress is about the kind: a coin minted today was never traded before, and the theorem is not about that coin.

The regress runs back through what happened, so there is a record of it: the exchanges made on each day, with days counted from a first one.

The claim. The hinge of the theorem is a sentence about judgment: the man considering money "cannot form a judgment about the future purchasing power of money otherwise than by looking at its configuration in the immediate past", and so "A medium of exchange without a past is unthinkable" (ch. XVII, §§4, 8). Mises's premise shuts every other ground of judgment out, and the library does not follow him that far. The docstring says what it asserts and, under Does not say, what it leaves out.

Appraisal from the past, weak form — nobody appraises a good as a medium of exchange on a day unless that good changed hands, against anything, on some earlier day.

Asserted of one record of exchanges and one good. "Appraises as a medium" is a belief that the good serves an onward end (MediumFrame.AppraisesAsMedium), so the claim is about what any prospective acquirer or holder can believe, not only about acquirers.

Source: Mises, Human Action, ch. XVII, §4: "he who considers acquiring or giving away money is, of course, first of all interested in its future purchasing power and the future structure of prices. But he cannot form a judgment about the future purchasing power of money otherwise than by looking at its configuration in the immediate past." Ch. XVII, §8: "A medium of exchange without a past is unthinkable." Ch. XVII, §1: "For all that is to be predicated of money is valid for every medium of exchange." Rothbard, MES p. 275: "Demand for a good as a medium of exchange must be predicated on a previously existing array of prices in terms of other goods." Rothbard, Power and Market (MES ed. p. 1021): a money must be one that "can be exchanged for a previously existing money".

Status: explicit-in-tradition as doctrine; the weak form is our-reconstruction. Mises states the premise exclusively — the judgment rests on the good's own price, from the immediate past, and on nothing else. This form keeps only that an exchange of the good came first, on any earlier day and against any good, money included.

Does not say:

  1. That the earlier exchange was on the immediately preceding day.

  2. That the appraisal rests on that exchange, or on that exchange alone — not that promises, edicts or cross rates play no part. Without quantities the vocabulary cannot say "rests on"; see History.OwnPastPrice.

  3. That the earlier exchange was barter (History.FormedInBarter).

  4. That the earlier acquirer wanted the good for itself; the regress derives that, from further conditions.

  5. Anything about day 0. That nobody appraises the good as a medium on the first day of the record is the separate condition History.BeginsBeforeMedium, the termination Mises asserts.

  6. That the record is complete. The claim is asserted of the record it is handed: an earlier exchange that happened but is missing from that record does not satisfy it.

One claim, and two conditions besides the hypothesis the sentence itself states. Neither condition is in Mises's sentence.

The first is that the regress ends. Counting days from a first one supplies half of that: from any day there are finitely many days back. The other half is that on the first day of the record the good is not yet a medium, and that is assumed. Both authors assert it; neither argues it.

The record begins before the medium — a condition on the situation. On day 0 nobody appraises m as a medium. Mises: "the regression does not go back endlessly" (Human Action, ch. XVII, §4); Rothbard: "as we regress backwards in time, we must eventually arrive at the original point when people first began to use gold as a medium of exchange" (MES p. 272). The second is that whoever acquired the good wanted it for something. The proof needs it at every step back: an acquirer who believed the good served no end at all, not even resale, is neither a holder of a medium nor a direct user, and the regress stops at him with nothing to show. We found no passage in either author that states it.

At each step back the proof meets an acquirer and the end he wanted the good for, and has to know which kind of end it is: onward, in which case the regress goes on, or not, in which case it has arrived. The negative form never has to decide. The positive form does, and nothing in the vocabulary or in constructive logic decides it. What decides it is a sentence of Mises's own: "Thus the demand for a medium of exchange is the composite of two partial demands: the demand displayed by the intention to use it in consumption and production and that displayed by the intention to use it as a medium of exchange". Read as saying that the two parts leave nothing over, the composite is the statement that each end is onward or is not — a case of the law of excluded middle, and the library takes no such law for granted. So it enters as a named claim rather than as logic. The sentence is Mises's; reading "composite" as leaving nothing over is ours.

Two partial demands — every end a good can serve is either one reached by passing the good on, or not: the demand for a medium of exchange divides without remainder into the two demands Mises names.

Asserted of one frame and one good. Its content is excluded middle for the one predicate Onward m: the vocabulary cannot decide it, and nothing in logic supplies it constructively, so where a theorem needs the split, the split is this claim.

Source: Mises, Human Action, ch. XVII, §4: "Thus the demand for a medium of exchange is the composite of two partial demands: the demand displayed by the intention to use it in consumption and production and that displayed by the intention to use it as a medium of exchange."

Status: explicit-in-tradition as doctrine; the reading of "composite" as an exhaustive sort of ends is our-reconstruction.

Does not say:

  1. That the non-onward part is consumption or production. Mises names them; this claim leaves the sort "not onward", and the narrow reading is the separate condition History.OtherEmploymentsNarrow.

  2. That the two demands add up, or have sizes at all. No quantities.

  3. That an agent can tell which kind an end is, or that the sort can be computed. It says each end IS one or the other.

  4. That excluded middle holds for any other predicate.

The prior money relates back — Power and Market's parenthesis: if anyone appraises m as a medium on day n, someone acquired m on an earlier day k against some good g; and if g was then a medium, someone acquired g before k for an end other than passing it on.

The claim and the conditions are carried for every good, since which good was paid is found, not given: the record must begin before any medium at all.

The detour through the prior money is not needed for the new medium. Under the broad reading the positive form already concludes, for the new medium itself, an earlier acquisition for an end other than passing it on, whatever was paid for it. The regress follows the acquirers' ends, never what they gave. The prior money's history adds a conclusion about the prior money.

"Its own past price." Mises's exclusive premise — that the appraisal rests on the good's own past price — would best be a condition separate from the claim, so that a failure could be laid to one or the other. Without quantities it cannot be kept separate. "Rests on" has no statement here; what can be stated is that an exchange of the good came earlier, and that is the claim again.

Own past price follows from the claim — stated, as it must be here, without quantities and without "rests on", the condition is the claim restricted to the days before n.

The case. The library builds one frame to test this on: a good first believed fit only for eating and, from the next day, also for passing on. In it both claims and every condition of the barter and narrow theorems hold together with a medium, so the theorems do not hold merely because nothing meets their hypotheses. The test is thin, and it is worth saying where: there is one agent, the good is exchanged against itself, and nothing in the frame is ranked.

What a failure would point at. Suppose a good is a medium, and no earlier acquisition of it for an end other than passing it on is to be found. Then the record began inside a monetary economy, with the good already a medium on its first day; or someone took the good believing it good for nothing; or the record is missing an exchange that happened; or the first claim failed, and someone appraised the good as a medium when it had never changed hands — on a promise, say, or an edict.

The second claim is not on that list. No case can be brought against it: that no end falls outside both parts is proved above, with no claim at all. What it adds is the right to say which part. Without it the negative form still stands.

Buying a good to resell it is not on that list as an escape. A man who holds a good in order to sell it believes it serves an onward end, so he already appraises it as a medium, and the regress asks for an acquisition earlier than his.

For a medium whose first buyers paid money, the versions come apart. Rothbard's barter version is silent: its condition fails wherever the good was bought with money before the day in question. The broad theorem is not silent on that account, since it never asks what was paid. Given its own two conditions, it asks whether some early acquirer wanted the good for an end other than passing it on, which is a question about those acquirers. The narrow theorem asks more — that what they wanted it for was consumption or production — and is silent where the good was first wanted for a game, a cause, or a test.
