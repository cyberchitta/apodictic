1.13. The regression theorem🔗

The regression theorem is the fourth target. It is Mises's account of where a medium of exchange can come from: "no good can be employed for the function of a medium of exchange which at the very beginning of its use for this purpose did not have exchange value on account of other employments" (Human Action, ch. XVII, §4). It was chosen because a case is pressed against it. Some media came into use inside a monetary economy. Bitcoin is one of them. Their first buyers paid money for them. What the theorem requires of such a medium depends on how its terms are read. Here each reading is written separately in Lean. So a move from one reading to another is a change of hypothesis, and the change shows in a signature. The end of the page says what each reading asks of a medium whose first buyers paid money.

The theorem has two halves, and only one is here. It is the genetic half, about a medium's origin. The other half says that today's purchasing power is formed from yesterday's. That half is about quantities, and nothing below has any. And the theorem is about any medium of exchange, not only money: "For all that is to be predicated of money is valid for every medium of exchange" (ch. XVII, §1).

A medium, by belief. Mises's medium of exchange is a good wanted "to keep it as a medium of exchange and to give it away at need in a later act of exchange" (ch. XVII, §4). To say so, the vocabulary grows by one bare relation (Onward). An end is onward for a good when the man would reach it by giving that good itself away in a later trade.

Onward m e: the agent would reach the end e by giving m itself away in a later trade. The relation has no properties.

A man appraises a good as a medium when he believes it serves some onward end (MediumFrame.AppraisesAsMedium). Two other definitions were available. Neither is used:

  • One calls a good a medium when its price is being appraised. Then a medium would have a price behind it by the meaning of the word. The regress would be true by definition.

  • The other waits for an actual later trade. But take a man who holds a good meaning to pass it on. He already demands it as a medium, whether or not the trade comes.

So demand in anticipation counts.

A good, here, is a kind, such as gold or salt. It is not a unit of a kind. The regress is about the kind. A coin minted today was never traded before, and the theorem is not about that coin.

The regress runs back through what happened. So there is a record of it (History). The record holds the exchanges made on each day. Days are counted from a first one.

The record of exchanges that happened. On day k, at time date k, the exchanges in exchanges k were made.

Shape (audit): the day of an exchange is its index here. Each party's beliefs about it are read at date k. The time fields of the exchange's two trades are not consulted. Nothing says the record is complete. The claim about the record (AppraisalFromPast) allows an appraisal of a good as a medium after day 0 only where the record shows an earlier exchange of that good. A record that leaves exchanges out allows no more such appraisals, and may allow fewer. So the claim, asserted of an incomplete record, can say more than it does of the complete record.

Three things are read off the record. The theorem comes in two forms, a negative and a positive, stated in these terms under The regress below:

  • A man acquired the good on a day, giving another good for it (History.AcquiredAgainst). Most of the conditions below are about these acquisitions.

Indirect exchange needs no new claim. Take a man who trades for a good only to pass it on. He ranks what he expects to reach by passing it on above what the good he gave could do. That is the exchange theorem again, with onward ends counted like any others (Regression.indirect_ranks_onward_ends). It carries the same claim and conditions as the exchange theorem. It adds one condition: that he wants the good for nothing else (Trade.PurelyIndirect). No theorem of the regress below uses it. It shows that a trade for a medium is already covered by the exchange theorem.

The claim. The hinge of the theorem is a sentence about judgment. The man considering money "cannot form a judgment about the future purchasing power of money otherwise than by looking at its configuration in the immediate past". And so "A medium of exchange without a past is unthinkable" (ch. XVII, §§4, 8). Mises's premise shuts out every other ground of judgment. The library does not follow him that far. The claim is appraisal from the past (AppraisalFromPast). Its docstring says what the claim asserts. Under Does not say, it says what the claim leaves out.

Appraisal from the past, weak form — nobody appraises a good as a medium of exchange on a day unless that good changed hands, against anything, on some earlier day.

It is asserted of one record of exchanges and one good. "Appraises as a medium" is a belief that the good serves an onward end (MediumFrame.AppraisesAsMedium). So the claim is about what any prospective acquirer or holder can believe. It is not only about acquirers.

Source: Mises, Human Action, ch. XVII, §4: "he who considers acquiring or giving away money is, of course, first of all interested in its future purchasing power and the future structure of prices. But he cannot form a judgment about the future purchasing power of money otherwise than by looking at its configuration in the immediate past." Ch. XVII, §8: "A medium of exchange without a past is unthinkable." Ch. XVII, §1: "For all that is to be predicated of money is valid for every medium of exchange." Rothbard, MES p. 275: "Demand for a good as a medium of exchange must be predicated on a previously existing array of prices in terms of other goods." Rothbard, Power and Market (MES ed. p. 1021): a money must be one that "can be exchanged for a previously existing money".

Status: explicit-in-tradition as doctrine; the weak form is our-reconstruction. Mises states the premise exclusively. The judgment rests on the good's own price, from the immediate past, and on nothing else. This form keeps only that an exchange of the good came first. That exchange may be on any earlier day, and against any good, money included.

Does not say:

  1. That the earlier exchange was on the immediately preceding day.

  2. That the appraisal rests on that exchange, or on that exchange alone. Nor that pledges, edicts or cross rates play no part. The vocabulary has no relation for "rests on". See History.OwnPastPrice.

  3. That the earlier exchange was barter (History.FormedInBarter).

  4. That the earlier acquirer wanted the good for itself, for an end other than passing it on. From further conditions, the regression theorem's positive form derives something weaker: someone, on some earlier day, acquired it so (Apodictic.Regression.direct_acquisition_before).

  5. Anything about day 0. That nobody appraises the good as a medium on the first day of the record is a separate condition, History.BeginsBeforeMedium. It is the termination Mises asserts.

  6. That the record is complete. The claim is asserted of the record it is handed. Say an earlier exchange happened but is missing from that record. Then the claim cannot use that exchange.

Rothbard's gold. Rothbard tells the regress with gold (MES, pp. 272–74). It is the example used below. The manifest holds one claim and three conditions. The third (noDirect) is the hypothesis Mises's sentence itself states. The other two conditions are not in his sentence.

The proof steps back one acquisition at a time. Each step rests on the claim and the conditions:

  • Someone appraises the good as a medium on a day after the first. The claim (AppraisalFromPast) gives an exchange of the good on an earlier day. So someone acquired it on that day.

  • The second condition (History.AcquisitionsPurposive, below) gives an end he acquired it for.

  • Say that end is onward. Then he believed the good served an onward end. So he appraised it as a medium on that earlier day, and the same step applies to him.

  • Say that end is not onward. Then his was an acquisition for an end other than passing the good on. The third condition (noDirect) says there was none.

  • The steps cannot go on for ever. Days are counted from a first one. The first condition (History.BeginsBeforeMedium, below) says nobody appraises the good as a medium on that first day.

The claim (AppraisalFromPast) fails if someone appraises the good as a medium on a day after the first, and the good changed hands on no earlier day of the record. In the example it holds, on our reading. Gold changed hands in barter on days 1 to 3.

The first condition is that the regress ends (History.BeginsBeforeMedium). Counting days from a first one supplies half of that. From any day there are finitely many days back. The other half is assumed: on the first day of the record, the good is not yet a medium. Both authors assert it. Neither argues it. In the example, the first day is Rothbard's day 1, a day of barter. The condition fails if someone appraises the good as a medium on the record's first day. A record of gold that began on day 4 would fail it.

The record begins before the medium — a condition on the situation. On day 0, nobody appraises m as a medium.

Mises: "the regression does not go back endlessly" (Human Action, ch. XVII, §4). Rothbard: "as we regress backwards in time, we must eventually arrive at the original point when people first began to use gold as a medium of exchange" (MES p. 272). Both assert it. Neither argues it.

It fails of a record that starts with m already a medium: someone appraises m as a medium on day 0.

The second condition is that whoever acquired the good wanted it for something (History.AcquisitionsPurposive). The proof needs it at every step back. Take an acquirer who believed the good served no end at all, not even resale. He neither holds a medium nor uses the good directly. So the regress stops at him with nothing to show. We found no passage in either author that states the condition. It fails if someone acquired the good before the day in question, believing it served no end at all. In the example, that is someone who took gold before day 4 believing it good for nothing. Rothbard's story leaves no room for him if "used only for direct consumption or production purposes" (p. 274) covers every acquisition. That reading is ours.

At each step back, the proof meets an acquirer and the end he wanted the good for. If the end is onward, the regress goes on. If it is not, the regress has arrived. The negative form never has to decide which. It concludes a denial: nobody appraises the good as a medium. So it is enough that each case leads to a contradiction. A non-onward end contradicts its third condition (noDirect). An onward end makes the acquirer an appraiser on an earlier day, and the same argument rules that out. Ruling out both cases needs no decision between them. The positive form must hand over the acquisition. So at each step it must know which case it is in. Nothing in the vocabulary decides that. A sentence of Mises's own decides it: "Thus the demand for a medium of exchange is the composite of two partial demands: the demand displayed by the intention to use it in consumption and production and that displayed by the intention to use it as a medium of exchange". Read the two parts as leaving nothing over. Then the composite is the statement that each end is onward or is not. That statement is a case of the law of excluded middle. With excluded middle, every question would count as having a yes-or-no answer, whether or not anything settles it (see The manifest, on Classical.choice). The library's theorems do without it. The one exception is the file of small frames (Consistency.lean; see The Result). Proofs that do without excluded middle are called constructive. So the statement enters as a named claim (TwoPartialDemands), not as logic. The sentence is Mises's. Reading "composite" as leaving nothing over is ours.

Two partial demands — every end is either one reached by passing the good m on, or not. That is, the demand for a medium of exchange divides without remainder into the two demands Mises names.

It is asserted of one frame and one good. Its content is excluded middle for the one predicate Onward m: each end is onward for m or is not. Classical logic would grant that for free. The theorems that need the split are proved constructively and do not assume it, and the vocabulary cannot decide it. So where a theorem needs the split, the split is this claim.

Source: Mises, Human Action, ch. XVII, §4: "Thus the demand for a medium of exchange is the composite of two partial demands: the demand displayed by the intention to use it in consumption and production and that displayed by the intention to use it as a medium of exchange."

Status: explicit-in-tradition as doctrine. The reading of "composite" as an exhaustive sort of ends is our-reconstruction.

Does not say:

  1. That the non-onward part is consumption or production. Mises names them. This claim leaves the sort "not onward". The narrow reading is the separate condition History.OtherEmploymentsNarrow.

  2. That the two demands add up, or have sizes at all. No quantities.

  3. That an agent can tell which kind an end is. Nor that the sort can be computed. It says each end IS one or the other.

  4. That excluded middle holds for any other predicate.

One thing is not proved in either form: that there was a last day of barter. That is a day before which the good was no medium, and after which it was. Rothbard's story has one. It is day 3 of his figure (p. 274): "the last day of barter, the day before gold began to be used as a medium of exchange" (p. 273). Proving a last day needs one thing decided for each day: whether the good is a medium on that day. The claim (TwoPartialDemands) decides less. It decides, for each end, whether that end is onward. Whether the good is a medium on a day asks something else: whether some agent, that day, believes it serves some onward end (History.MediumAt). That is a question about every agent and every end at once. No claim here settles it. It would take excluded middle for that question too. The claim grants excluded middle for no other question (its Does not say, item 4).

Two readings of "other employments". In the theorems above, an end other than passing the good on is any such end. The direct part of the demand is whatever is not onward. Mises names something narrower: "the services it can render directly to consumption or production". He does not say which ends those are. And the library sorts no ends. So the narrow reading is a condition (History.OtherEmploymentsNarrow). Whoever applies the theorem supplies the ends he counts as consumption or production. The condition says that every other employment the good was acquired for was one of them. It fails if someone acquired the good before the day in question for an end that is not onward and is outside them. In the example, that is someone who took gold before day 4 for a game, say. For day 4, in Rothbard's story it holds: in the days of barter gold is used "only for direct consumption or production purposes" (p. 274). Reading his "purposes" as the acquirers' ends is ours.

Rothbard's two versions. Rothbard states the origin twice, and differently:

  • In MES, a medium "can arise only out of a commodity previously used directly in a barter situation, and therefore having had an array of prices in terms of other goods" (p. 275).

  • In Power and Market, "no money can be established on the market except as it can be exchanged for a previously existing money (which in turn must have ultimately related back to a commodity in barter)" (MES ed., p. 1021).

The first version adds a clause to the conclusion. The clause is that the earlier acquisition, for an end other than passing the good on, was made against a good not then a medium. The library does not derive that clause from its claims. It takes it as a condition on every earlier acquisition of the good (History.FormedInBarter). The conclusion's clause is that condition, applied to the one acquisition the regress finds. In the example, we read Rothbard's days 1 to 3 as having no medium at all. Then nothing given for gold before day 4 was a medium, and the condition holds. It fails if, on some day before the day in question, the good was acquired against a good that someone then appraised as a medium. A good bought with money is one case:

The second version is a theorem about the prior money (Regression.prior_money_relates_back). Rothbard's case is Germany after its inflation of 1923. It could found "a new currency based on exchanges with other pre-existing moneys (gold or foreign paper)" (p. 1021). The theorem runs as follows. Say the new medium is a medium on some day. Then it was acquired, on an earlier day, against some good. Say that good was then a medium itself. Then, before that day, someone acquired that good for an end other than passing it on. This delivers less than the parenthesis says. It gives an earlier acquisition of that good for an end of its own. It does not give that the acquisition was in barter. And the theorem carries both claims, and the two conditions on the record's start and on purposive acquisitions (History.BeginsBeforeMedium, History.AcquisitionsPurposive), for every good. Its docstring says why.

The prior money relates back — Power and Market's parenthesis. Say anyone appraises m as a medium on day n. Then someone acquired m on an earlier day k, against some good g. And say g was then a medium. Then someone acquired g before k, for an end other than passing it on.

Both claims, and the conditions begins and purposive, are carried for every good. The statement does not name the good paid for m. The proof finds it in the record. So the hypotheses are needed of whatever good it finds. The statement carries them for every good, which covers any good it might find. In particular begins then holds of every good: on day 0, nobody appraises any good as a medium.

The detour through the prior money is not needed for the new medium. Take the broad reading. The positive form already concludes, for the new medium itself, an earlier acquisition for an end other than passing it on. It concludes this whatever was paid for the new medium. The regress follows the acquirers' ends, never what they gave. The prior money's history adds a conclusion about the prior money.

"Its own past price." Mises's exclusive premise is that the appraisal rests on the good's own past price. That premise would best be a condition separate from the claim. Then a failure could be laid to one or the other. Here it cannot be kept separate. The vocabulary has no quantities: no prices, no purchasing power. An appraisal is a belief that the good serves an onward end (MediumFrame.AppraisesAsMedium). It has no size. Nothing in the vocabulary links it to an exchange it was formed from. So "rests on" has no statement here. What can be stated is that an exchange of the good came earlier (History.OwnPastPrice). And that is the claim again (Regression.ownPastPrice_of_claim).

The case. The library builds one frame to test this on. In it, a good is first believed fit only for eating. From the next day, it is also believed fit for passing on. In this frame both claims hold. So does every condition of the positive barter and narrow theorems. The negative forms are not tried on this frame. And on the second day someone appraises the good as a medium. So the theorems do not hold merely because nothing meets their hypotheses. The test is thin, and it is worth saying where:

  • There is one agent. So the acquirer the regress finds is the same man who appraises the good as a medium.

  • The good is exchanged against itself, once a day. So nothing but the good itself is ever given for it. The barter theorem is tried for the second day. So its condition (History.FormedInBarter) looks only at the first day's exchange. It holds only because the good is no medium on that day. The prior-money theorem (Regression.prior_money_relates_back) is not tried on this frame.

  • Nothing in the frame is ranked. So no theorem that concludes a ranking is tried here, the indirect-exchange theorem (Regression.indirect_ranks_onward_ends) among them.

What a failure would point at. Suppose a good is a medium. And no earlier acquisition of it, for an end other than passing it on, is to be found. Then one of these happened:

  • The record began inside a monetary economy, with the good already a medium on its first day (History.BeginsBeforeMedium).

  • Someone took the good believing it good for nothing (History.AcquisitionsPurposive).

  • The record is missing an exchange that happened. A missing exchange can make only the claim fail: the claim is the one hypothesis that asks the record for an exchange. Then the claim fails of the record. It may still hold of the complete record.

  • The first claim failed (AppraisalFromPast). Someone appraised the good as a medium when it had never changed hands. He might have done so on an issuer's pledge, say, or an edict.

The second claim (TwoPartialDemands) is not on that list. No case can be brought against it. In the regress, a case would be an end the good serves that is in neither part. The double negation above shows, with no claim at all, that there is no such end (Regression.partial_demands_not_not_exhaust). The regress looks only at ends the good is believed to serve. What the claim adds is the right to say which part. Without it, the negative form still stands.

Buying a good to resell it is not on that list as an escape. A man who holds a good in order to sell it believes it serves an onward end. So he already appraises it as a medium. And the regress asks for an acquisition earlier than his.

For a medium whose first buyers paid money, the versions come apart:

  • Rothbard's barter version is silent. Its condition fails wherever the good was bought with money before the day in question.

  • The broad theorem is not silent on that account, since it never asks what was paid. Say the record begins before the good is a medium (History.BeginsBeforeMedium), and every acquisition was for some end (History.AcquisitionsPurposive). Then it asks a question about the early acquirers. Did some early acquirer want the good for an end other than passing it on? Say the record shows none before a day on which the good is a medium. Then, by the negative form, the claim (AppraisalFromPast) failed. It failed of the record. If the record is complete, it failed of the complete record too. If the record misses an exchange, the failure may be the record's alone.

  • The narrow theorem asks more: that what they wanted it for was consumption or production. It is silent where the good was first wanted for a game, a cause, or a test.

This page does not answer these questions for Bitcoin. Nor does it say whether the claim fails for it.
