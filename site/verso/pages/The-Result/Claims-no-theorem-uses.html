1.17. Claims no theorem uses🔗

Terms such as library, claim and frame are explained under The Result.

One statement at the centre of the doctrine is carried by no theorem here. It is that there is any action at all. The library writes it down as a definition (Mises.ActionOccurs). A definition only names the statement. It does not assume it true. No claim in Praxeology.lean asserts it, and no theorem takes it as a hypothesis. So it is not one of the claims under The claims. A claim is added there only when some theorem first needs it.

The doctrine that people act splits into three here (see The other findings). The existence statement above is one of the three. The other two, demonstrated preference and the definition of action, follow in turn.

Demonstrated preference in Rothbard's sense (DemonstratedPreference) is the bridge from what a man actually does to what he prefers. It is one of the claims under The claims. Rothbard states it of any action. Here it is asserted only of the one trade a theorem is handed. So the claim does not say that every act demonstrates preference. Theorems in three parts carry it:

  • Exchange (MutualBenefit.reverse_valuations and the other exchange theorems).

  • The regression theorem (Regression.indirect_ranks_onward_ends).

  • Interest (PureInterest.struck_rate_positive).

Action itself is a definition here (Action). No derivation of the law of marginal utility uses it. The law is quoted under The horses. Each author's argument for the law is here called a route (see The Result). On each route, the claim the law needs is about a plan: what the man would do with every part of his stock, settled in advance. It is not about anything he does. The case is made under The finding.

Other theorems do take an action. Two theorems under Time preference do (TimePreference.consumption_reveals, TimePreference.regress). The claim they carry, demonstrated time preference (DemonstratedTimePreference), is stated of one action.

The existence statement appears in one test: of Mises's dilemma. The dilemma is his argument for the law, set out under Mises's route. Its second horn is that there could be only one action. Mises rejects that horn by appeal to the existence statement. A frame with exactly one action (Model.Solitary) tests the appeal. In that frame there is action, and there is at most one action. So the existence statement cannot rule out that there is only one action. What his step needs is that action is plural: that two distinct actions occur (Mises.ActionRecurs). The library names that too as a definition and asserts it nowhere. One theorem takes it as a hypothesis and refutes the second horn with it (Mises.recurs_refutes_second_horn).

🔗structure
Apodictic.Action (praxis : ActionFrame) : Type
Apodictic.Action (praxis : ActionFrame) :
  Type

An action. An agent, at a time, uses means. He believes the means will bring about a chosen end. By acting he gives up at least one alternative end.

The fields hold together as a package. Nothing with fewer parts counts as an action. The first five fields are the parts. The last three are requirements the parts must meet. But this is a definition, so it asserts nothing. Shape: our-reconstruction. That is, the choice of fields that set out an action, and their types, is ours.

No derivation of the law of marginal utility uses this structure. On both routes, Rothbard's and Mises's, the claim the law needs is about the counterfactual plan (SwapDominant, ServedInOrder). The plan says, for each set of the agent's units, which ends he would serve with exactly those units, settled in advance. Neither claim is about anything he does. The structure does appear in Mises's dilemma: in the statement that there is action (Apodictic.Mises.ActionOccurs), and in the statement that two distinct actions occur (Apodictic.Mises.ActionRecurs). The dilemma is machine-checked not to deliver the law.
