1.10. The ladder as a history🔗

Terms such as library, claim and condition are explained under The Result.

The library derives the law of marginal utility by two routes: Rothbard's (see The theorems) and Mises's (see Mises's route). Mises states the law as a ladder, from a supply of n–1 units to n. His sentence is quoted below. Both routes read the ladder at one moment. Take Rothbard's man with six horses. The law compares the six he holds with five he might have held instead. He does not hold those five. So the comparison is with a supply he does not hold.

Both routes get that comparison from a plan (AllocationPlan). A sub-stock is a set of units cut from the man's stock. The plan says, for each sub-stock, which wants he would serve with exactly those units (see The vocabulary).

There is another way to read the ladder. Rothbard tells it that way himself: "suppose that the isolated individual successively finds one horse, then a second, then a third" (p. 24). Mises's ladder runs in the same direction, from n–1 units to n.

Read like that, the ladder is not about a plan at all. It is about two things the man actually did:

  • What he served when he held n–1 units.

  • What he serves once he holds n.

No stock he does not hold comes into it. Unlike the plan, nothing here says in advance what he would serve with each sub-stock of his stock.

The library carries this reading in a module of its own. The module imports the claims. It imports neither route's theorems. So it cannot borrow a result from either route. Whatever it spends shows on its theorem's signature. Its objects are these:

  • Two actual allocations (Allocation). Each records the wants he served with the stock he held at one moment.

  • A supply that grew by one unit from one moment to the other (Stock.Grows).

Both definitions are under The vocabulary. In the Lean, the two stocks are named earlier and later. The two allocations are named before (of earlier) and after (of later).

In place of a plan, the reading spends acting in order (ActsInOrder). It is the fourth claim under The claims. It is the only claim in the library that runs from what a man ranks to what he actually does. Here is what it says. With the stock he holds at one moment, he never serves a want while passing over one he prefers to it and believes the good can serve.

The question the reading forces. Rothbard goes on: "When the second horse is found, he will be put to work satisfying the most urgent of the wants remaining. These wants, however, must be ranked lower than the wants that the previous horse has satisfied." Ranked lower on which scale? The man ranks at every moment he acts. He ranked when he held one horse. He ranks again when he holds two. Rothbard's sentence does not say which ranking it means. Neither does Mises's. A proof assistant will not let that go unsaid. So the ladder (Temporal.Ladder) takes as an argument the moment whose scale does the ranking:

Mises's ladder as a history, judged on the scale of a stated time.

"If the supply available increases from n–1 units to n units, the increment can be employed only for the removal of a want which is less urgent or less painful than the least urgent or least painful among all those wants which could be removed by means of the supply n–1" (Human Action, ch. VII, §1).

Two phrases are read as history:

  • "could be removed by means of the supply n–1" is read as WAS removed by it, at the earlier time.

  • "the increment can be employed" is read as what the later allocation serves that the earlier did not.

Besides the two allocations, the definition takes a time, named judged. It is the time whose scale ranks the two wants. Mises's sentence does not say which. The choice matters. Judged on the earlier scale, the ladder is a theorem (ladder_judged_earlier). Judged on the later scale, it does not follow, even with acting in order, comparability and asymmetry granted at both times (LadderJudgedLaterFromOrder, shown false in Apodictic.Model.later_judged_ladder_fails).

The two answers come apart.

Judged on the earlier scale, the ladder is a theorem (Temporal.ladder_judged_earlier). It is a short one. Read it as acting in order restated about a want the next horse happens to serve. Do not read it as a route to the law. The manifest below, and what is missing from it, shows why.

Take Rothbard's horses. The man holds one horse at the earlier moment and two at the later. The theorem's conclusion is about the earlier scale, the one he ranked by when he held one horse. On that scale, the least urgent want he served then ranks above any want he serves with two horses and did not serve with one.

The theorem spends one claim and two conditions. Each can fail:

  • He acted in order with one horse (ActsInOrder before). Rothbard says so: action "uses scarce means to satisfy the most urgent of the not yet satisfied wants" (p. 24). This fails if he served a want while passing over one he preferred to it and believed a horse could serve.

  • At the earlier moment, any two wants a horse can serve were ranked one way or the other (earlier.ComparableServiceable). This fails if two of them were not ranked against each other at the earlier moment. Rothbard's story ranks the wants it names, but does not say every pair was ranked. The theorem adds it as a condition.

  • He learned no new use for a horse between the two moments (earlier.NoNewUses later). This fails if, by the second horse, he believes a horse can serve a want he did not believe it could serve before. Rothbard's story does not say either way. The theorem adds it as a condition.

The ladder, judged on the earlier scale — a theorem, from a claim about actual action and two situational conditions.

What it spends:

  • The earlier allocation acts in order (ActsInOrder before).

  • The good's serviceable ends are comparable at the earlier time (earlier.ComparableServiceable).

  • Whatever the good is believed to serve at the later time, it was believed to serve at the earlier one (earlier.NoNewUses later). Acting in order and comparability speak only of the wants the good was believed able to serve at the earlier time. No new uses puts the newly served want among them.

What it does NOT spend, as the signature shows:

  • Of the later allocation, nothing beyond which wants it serves.

  • Nothing about the supply having grown. Stock.Grows is not a hypothesis. Added as one, it fails the unused-argument linter.

  • Nothing about the order of the two times. "Earlier" and "later" are names only, not an order.

  • Nothing about the later scale.

The argument is Mises's. The newly served want was serviceable and unserved before. Comparability ranks it against a want served before, one way or the other. Had it ranked above, acting in order would have served it. So it ranks below.

Mises's sentence picks out "the least urgent or least painful among all those wants which could be removed by means of the supply n–1", read here as served before. The proof needs only that the compared want was served, not that it was the least urgent one served. So that qualifier is idle here. It is idle on the counterfactual readings too: the ladder from Rothbard's premises (Apodictic.Mises.ladder_holds) and from Mises's (Apodictic.Mises.ladder_from_order).

So this is the ladder as a statement about the EARLIER allocation. What was passed over then ranked below what was served then. Of the newly served want it says only that it was passed over then.

Read what is not on it:

  • The supply growing is not a hypothesis. Added as one, the linter refuses it as unused.

  • Nothing about the later scale appears.

  • Nothing about how the later allocation was chosen appears.

  • Nothing about which moment came first appears. In the Lean, "earlier" and "later" are names only, of the two stocks and their times. The frame these theorems use (ActionFrame) puts no order on its times.

The first absence is why it is not a route to the law. Mises's law speaks of an increment. This theorem never uses the supply's growth. So nothing in it depends on a unit having been added.

One condition does span the two moments: no new uses (earlier.NoNewUses later). Whatever the good is believed to serve later, it was believed to serve earlier. He may stop believing in a use. He may not start. The routes with a plan read at one moment, so one scale ranks everything. Here what spans the moments is a one-way condition on belief about the good, not the scale. Without the condition, the new horse's want need not be among the wants a horse was believed able to serve at the earlier moment. The theorem's other two hypotheses speak only of those wants: acting in order (ActsInOrder before) and comparability (earlier.ComparableServiceable). The condition is under The conditions with the rest.

It is a short theorem, and it should be read as one. What it says is about the earlier act. Whatever he passed over then ranked below whatever he served then. The want the second horse later goes to is simply one of the wants he passed over. The added unit is narration. On this reading, the ladder is acting in order, restated about a want the next horse happens to serve.

Judged on the later scale, it does not follow. Grant, at both moments, the one-moment claim and condition the earlier-scale theorem used, and one claim more:

  • He acts in order (ActsInOrder before, ActsInOrder after). This is a claim.

  • The good's wants are comparable (earlier.ComparableServiceable, later.ComparableServiceable). This is a condition.

  • Preference is asymmetric: he never prefers one bundle to another and the second to the first at the same moment (AsymmetricPreference). This is a claim, the third under The claims. The earlier-scale theorem does not need it.

Grant two things across the moments:

  • The supply grew by one (earlier.Grows later).

  • The good is believed able to serve the same wants at both moments (later.serves = earlier.serves). This is more than no new uses, which asks only that the wants the good is believed able to serve later be among those it was believed able to serve earlier.

Then ask for the ladder on the later scale (Temporal.LadderJudgedLaterFromOrder):

The ladder, judged on the later scale, from acting in order, comparability and asymmetry at both times — written out as a statement in its own right (in Lean, a Prop). That way it can be shown false: a small frame is built in which every hypothesis holds and the conclusion fails (Apodictic.Model.later_judged_ladder_fails).

Granted at BOTH times:

  • Acting in order.

  • Comparability of the good's ends.

  • Asymmetry of preference.

Granted across the two times:

  • The supply grew by one.

  • The good is believed to serve the same ends.

Not granted: any relation between the two scales. The statement is false in a frame where the scale reverses between the two times. Both authors say that situation cannot be excluded.

The later-judged ladder does not follow from one-time premises. Every hypothesis of Temporal.LadderJudgedLaterFromOrder holds in this frame. Its conclusion does not. What is missing is a relation between the two scales. This frame is exactly a case where there is none.

The frame that refutes it is small. There are two moments and two wants. He holds one horse, and then two. With one horse, he serves the want most urgent then. With two, he serves both. By then the other want has become the more urgent. Each act is in order on its own scale. On the later scale, the want newly served with two horses outranks the want served with one. Judged there, the ladder fails.

What would close the gap, and why it is not assumed. It would take a relation between the man's scales at the two moments. The later scale would rank what the earlier ranked, the same way. Call that constancy. Both authors deny it. Mises: "value judgments are not immutable" (Human Action, ch. V, §4). Rothbard's own actor changes his mind between moments, a few pages before the horses: "the ranking on his preference scale shifts to this order" (p. 18). So the history reading does not assume constancy. It assumes it neither as a claim nor as a condition. Where the reading would need it, the frame above shows what goes missing.

Rothbard, for his part, would not judge on the earlier scale at all. When his man must give up one of six horses, "he deals only with his presently available stock", in "disregard of past events" (p. 27). Judged that way, the ladder is the one-moment law at the later time: the supply he holds against one unit fewer. Both routes derive that law from a premise about a plan (SwapDominant, ServedInOrder; see The circle). So judging that way brings back the plan, and the circle with it.

Constancy is not all they deny. They also deny something that bears on the claim this reading does spend, acting in order (ActsInOrder). Both hold that a scale of value exists only as it is read off what a man does. So a scale and the act it was read from cannot disagree. The passages are quoted in ActsInOrder's docstring under The claims. The library keeps the ranking apart from the act. That way the claim can say something an act could contradict. That is why the claim's status is our reconstruction and not theirs.

The trade. The counterfactual routes need a plan that no act exhibits. Both authors deny a scale that stands apart from the concrete choices, and the plan is one (see The circle). Judged on the later scale, the history needs a scale that no act can show to have stayed put. Judged on the earlier scale, where it is a theorem, it needs a man who learned no new use for the good between the two acts (earlier.NoNewUses later). Neither author addresses that condition. Nothing in the texts grants it, and nothing refuses it. So the two routes pay in different coin:

  • The counterfactual routes rest on something their authors refuse.

  • The history route rests on something they never examined.

Reading the ladder as a history moves the cost. It does not remove it.

What a failure would point at. Suppose the ladder, read as a history, fails of a real case. Its manifest says where to look. Some conditions are built into its vocabulary: fields of the stocks and allocations it takes, such as each allocation serving only wants the good is believed able to serve. Whoever supplies a stock or an allocation has already met them (see The manifest). Besides those, it names three places:

  • The man did not act in order (ActsInOrder before).

  • He learned a new use for the good between the moments (earlier.NoNewUses later).

  • Two of the good's wants were never ranked against each other at the earlier moment (earlier.ComparableServiceable).

A scale that moved is not on the list. That is because no theorem on this reading assumes it stayed put. Those three are where to look.
