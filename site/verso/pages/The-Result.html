1. The Result🔗

What this is. This document rebuilds arguments of Mises and Rothbard in Lean 4. Lean is a proof assistant: a program that checks every step of a proof. The main case is the law of marginal utility. Later parts do the same for exchange, time preference, the regression theorem and interest. The aim is to find the premises the verbal arguments leave unstated.

Terms. These words are used in every part.

  • The library is the Lean code in which every step is checked.

  • A docstring is the text attached to a name in the library.

  • A theorem's signature lists everything it takes: its claims, its conditions, and the things they speak of, such as a man and his stock of goods.

  • A praxeological claim, or claim, is a premise meant to hold of all action, not only in some situations. In the library each claim is a structure: a named bundle of statements, kept in the file Praxeology.lean. See The claims.

  • A condition says something about the situation, not about action as such. Where one fails, the theorem says nothing. See The conditions.

  • The linter (#lint only unusedArguments) runs on every build, in every file whose theorems carry a claim or a condition. It fails the build when one of those theorems lists anything it never uses. An exception is a name marked with _. That mark records that the hypothesis does no work, and each such case is a finding (see The manifest).

  • A frame (ActionFrame) is a world of agents, goods, ends and times, with what each agent believes and prefers. The library builds some small frames. Some show that the claims and conditions can hold together. Others show that one premise does not give another.

  • The vocabulary is the library's terms, such as a man's stock of goods and his plan for it. See The vocabulary.

The result. Rothbard says the law of marginal utility was "deduced from the axiom of action" (MES p. 33). So one would expect it to need only claims about what people do. Each author gives one verbal argument for the law. Here these are called routes: Rothbard's in Man, Economy, and State (pp. 24–27), and Mises's in Human Action (ch. VII, §1; see Mises's route). On each route the proof of the law checks. On each it needs exactly one claim. That claim is not about action:

  • On Rothbard's route it is swap dominance (SwapDominant).

  • On Mises's route it is that the agent serves in order of urgency (ServedInOrder).

Each is about a plan: what the man would do with every part of his stock, settled in advance. Neither is about anything he does. A third reading of the law trades the plan for a claim about what he does (ActsInOrder). It proves less in return: the law only on the scale he ranked by before his stock grew. The case is made under The finding.

How to read it. Lean will not let a proof skip a step. A premise the argument needs must be written down, or the proof does not check. It goes into the theorem's own statement, or into a definition the statement names. So there is one fixed place to look. Read the statement, the definitions it names, and the definitions those name in turn. Every assumption the theorem rests on is there, or in Lean's logical background. The small frames, and every theorem about them, are in one file (Consistency.lean). For every theorem outside it, that background is two axioms of ordinary mathematics (propext, Quot.sound). It holds no claim about economics. Theorems in that file may also use a third (Classical.choice). No theorem outside that file depends on it.

The statement is also printed as a manifest. The project's command #manifest derives it from the theorem. It sorts what the statement lists into kinds: the claims, the conditions, and the things they speak of. Some conditions do not appear in the statement itself. They are fields of the structures the theorem takes as arguments, such as a man's stock of goods. Those structures are definitions the statement names. #manifest looks inside each of them that the library defines, and prints their conditions too. It looks one level deep. Anything deeper is in a definition that one of those names, and so on down. The law of marginal utility carries two such conditions:

  • Every unit of the stock is believed to serve exactly the ends the good can serve, by his lights (stock.unitsAlike).

  • Whatever units the man's plan is asked about, it would serve no end the good is not believed able to serve (plan.servesOnlyWhatItCan).

The line headed logical background is Lean's own. See The manifest.

Each docstring here is taken from the library each time this document is built. It is shown with the Lean signature it is attached to. Lean checks that signature. It does not check the docstring's words. Each manifest is printed by Lean during the same build. The prose around them is not checked by Lean.
